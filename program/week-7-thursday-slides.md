## 1. Design Techniques: Aperture and depth of field

**Documents:** `week-7/handouts/photo-worksheet.md` (Day 2 section),
`week-7/handouts/photo-worksheet-key.md` (teacher only)
**Run it as:** aperture and depth of field on the board for about fifteen minutes, then Challenges 4 to
6. Cameras stay in the room, shared.
**The one students get wrong:** the f-numbers run backwards. **Say it three times.**
**The hidden variable:** depth of field also depends on **distance**. A wide aperture with the
background two feet behind the subject barely blurs, and students will conclude the aperture did not
work. Walk the room and move them.

### Slide 1: Standards today

> Create one standards slide for a high school design class. Title: "Today's Competencies." List these
> with their FULL text, not just the numbers, in large readable type: 7.9.5 Use International Standards
> Organization (ISO), shutter speed, aperture, and white balance settings to shoot manual photographs.
> 7.9.4 Determine composition, formal qualities, scale, and use of space. 7.9.2 Select appropriate camera
> or device. Add a small line: "We are doing aperture today. White balance comes next week, in the
> editor." Design it to stay readable on the board all period. One slide.

### Slide 2: Today, tied to those competencies

> Create one agenda slide for a high school design class titled "Today." A table with two columns, WHAT
> WE ARE DOING and WHICH COMPETENCY. Rows: "Aperture priority: what's in focus and what isn't" / 7.9.5.
> "Challenge 4, shallow depth of field" / 7.9.5 and 7.9.4. "Challenge 5, deep depth of field" / 7.9.5.
> "Challenge 6, same subject both ways" / 7.9.4. Add a line: "Day 2 of the worksheet. Day 3 is Friday:
> rename, file, upload." One slide.

### Slide 3: Aperture, and the backwards numbers

> Create one concept slide for a high school photography class titled "Aperture." Define it as how wide
> the lens opens, written as an f-number. Then the key comparison in a large two-row layout: "f/3.5,
> SMALL number, WIDE open, lots of light, narrow slice in focus" and "f/22, BIG number, TINY opening,
> little light, nearly everything in focus." Then a box in very large text: "The numbers run BACKWARDS. A
> smaller number is a BIGGER opening. This is the thing everybody gets wrong." Add a small line: "Mode
> dial: Av. You pick the aperture, the camera picks the shutter speed." One slide.

### Slide 4: Depth of field

> Create one concept slide for a high school photography class titled "Depth Of Field." Define it: how
> much of the photo is in focus, front to back. Show two labeled illustrations side by side: SHALLOW,
> subject sharp with a melted background, labeled "portraits, isolating a detail, making someone stand
> out from clutter"; and DEEP, everything sharp front to back, labeled "landscapes, group shots, anywhere
> the background matters." Then a box: "Aperture is not the only thing that controls this. DISTANCE
> matters too. Get CLOSE to your subject and put real distance BEHIND it, or a wide aperture won't look
> like much." One slide.

### Slide 5: Today's three challenges

> Create one assignment slide for a high school photography class titled "Mode Dial: Av." Three numbered
> challenges, each with a RANGE to try: "4. SHALLOW. One subject close to you, something well behind it.
> Try f/3.5, f/4.5, f/5.6. 5. DEEP. Something with a clear front, middle and back. Try f/8, f/11, f/16,
> f/22. Watch what shutter speed the camera picks as you close down. 6. BOTH WAYS. One subject, two
> photos, widest and narrowest. Nothing else changes." Then a box: "At f/22 indoors the camera will pick
> a slow shutter and your photos will shake. That's not a mistake, that's question 19." One slide.

## 2. Video & Sound: Log it, mark it

**Documents:** `week-7/handouts/cut-plan-and-storyboard.md`, `week-7/project.md`
**Run it as:** work period. Watch every clip, log it **with timestamps**, start marking cut points on
the script.
**Say the gate out loud:** a complete, clear storyboard is what unlocks CapCut next week. Whoever
finishes the plan first starts editing first.
**Before Tuesday:** decide how the long files get split. **CapCut has no media-panel subclips**, so the
options are right-click, **Split Scenes** (auto-detects, lands on the timeline, test it first), splitting
on the timeline normally, or pre-splitting outside CapCut with something like LosslessCut.

### Slide 1: Standards today

> Create one standards slide for a high school video production class. Title: "Today's Standards." List
> these with their FULL text, not just the numbers, in large readable type: 7.2.4 Determine uses and
> needs for site maps, multimedia scripts, storyboards, and flowcharts. 7.7.6 Add transitions (dissolves,
> wipes, cuts), titles, special effects, and digital effects. 7.3.7 Edit video footage. One slide.

### Slide 2: Log it with timestamps

> Create one work slide for a high school video production class titled "Log Every Clip." Explain they
> are writing down, for each clip: the file name; the timestamp in and out, like 0:42 to 1:15; which
> scene it belongs to; which angle it is, wide, medium, close-up, or over the shoulder; and whether it is
> usable. Then a box in large text: "If your project came as ONE LONG FILE, the timestamps ARE the
> assignment. You are marking where that file gets cut apart. Write down every point where a new shot or
> angle starts." Add a line: "Watch every clip. The one you skip is the cutaway you need tomorrow." One
> slide.

### Slide 3: The gate

> Create one slide for a high school video production class. In very large text in the middle: "Show me
> a complete, clear storyboard and you can start editing." Below it: "Until then, I don't want to see
> CapCut open." Then, smaller: "This isn't about speed and it isn't a punishment. Whoever finishes the
> plan first starts editing first. If your storyboard is done Monday, you edit Monday." Then a short
> list headed "Complete means": every shot in order; framing visible; characters tellable apart; the cut
> type written between panels. One slide.

### Board version

## 3. Aviation UAS: Orbit or figure eight

**Documents:** `week-7/handouts/orbit-drill.md` (teacher reference, bring it outside)
**Run it as:** preflight gate, then assign the drill **by name, quietly**, off last week's log.
**One question decides it:** can they fly a clean figure eight without losing orientation at the
crossover?
**Not announced as levels.** A pilot who orbits cleanly moves to the figure eight the same day.

### Slide 1: Standards today

> Create one standards slide for a high school aviation and drone class. Title: "Today's Competencies."
> List these with their FULL text, not just the numbers, in large readable type: 7.12 Perform takeoff,
> flight, and landing operations. 7.4.2 Describe the forces of flight and the three axes of motion. 2.1
> Apply safety practices. Add a short line: "Today you fly the thing you drew yesterday." One slide.

### Slide 2: The gate

> Create one preflight slide for a high school drone class titled "No Preflight, No Flight." A checklist
> in large text: METAR read out loud; compared against your personal minimums; go or no-go call made,
> with the deciding number named; airspace confirmed; preflight walked item by item, out loud; roles
> assigned, Pilot, Visual Observer, Logger; everyone else behind the spectator line. Add a box: "A no-go
> is a real outcome and it gets logged like any other." One slide.

### Slide 3: Two drills

> Create one slide for a high school drone class titled "Two Drills." Two columns. Left, "ORBIT A
> TARGET": pick a tree, pole or cone; circle it at 15 feet, about 20 feet out; keep the gap to it the
> same the whole way around; nose stays pointed one direction, no yaw yet; one lap each direction. Right,
> "FIGURE EIGHT, OPEN AIR": two even loops crossing in the middle; 15 feet, constant; nose fixed; the
> CROSSOVER is the whole drill, the aircraft should stop turning and go straight for a beat before the
> next loop. Then a bar across the bottom: "Judged on ROUND, LEVEL, and CONTINUOUS. Not speed. Nobody is
> timing you." Add a line: "Visual Observer: call out 'closer' and 'wider' as it goes around." One slide.

## 4. Middle School CS: Bell ringer, then work

**Documents:** `week-7/handouts/bell-ringers.md` (teacher reference)
**Run it as:** bell ringer, three to five minutes plus going over it, then work time. Friday is also a
work day.

### Slide 1: Bell ringer

> Create one bell ringer slide for a middle school computer science class. Big title: "Bell Ringer."
> Show this as indented block-style pseudocode in large monospace text: "on start" then indented "repeat
> 5 times" then further indented "set mySprite to sprite of kind Enemy", then a blank line, then "set
> count to 0" then "for element value of  sprites of kind Enemy" then indented "change count by 1".
> Below it the question: "What is count at the end?" Then a box: "Finished? Change ONE number so count
> ends at 8." Do not show the answer. One slide.

### Slide 2: Where did the number come from

> Create one slide for a middle school computer science class titled "Nobody Typed That Number." Body in
> large text: "sprites of kind Enemy hands back a LIST of every enemy on the screen right now. The loop
> runs once per item in that list." Then the key line, larger: "The number of sprites on the screen
> decides how many times the loop runs." Then a short question: "What happens if a sprite gets destroyed
> before the second loop runs?" with the answer underneath in smaller text: "The list is shorter, so the
> loop runs fewer times. The list is LIVE." One slide.

### Board version

```
BELL RINGER                        (not graded)
  on start
      repeat 5 times
          set mySprite to sprite of kind Enemy

      set count to 0
      for element value of  sprites of kind Enemy
          change count by 1

  what is count at the end?
  done? change ONE number so count ends at 8.

NOBODY TYPED THAT NUMBER.
  "sprites of kind Enemy" hands back a LIST of every
  enemy on the screen RIGHT NOW.

  -> the number of sprites DECIDES how many times it runs

  -> shorter list -> fewer runs. THE LIST IS LIVE.
```
