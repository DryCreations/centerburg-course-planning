## 1. Design Techniques: Reading the histogram, together

**Documents:** **`week-8/handouts/tone-lab.md`** (today's sheet, post it with a set of photos to choose
from), **`week-8/teacher-notes.md` (the run of show, read it first)**
**The slides are the teaching surface today, not Camera Raw.** Every histogram shape they need to
recognize is drawn on a slide, so you are pointing at a diagram and they are comparing it to their own
screen. **You do not have to drive Camera Raw while explaining.**
**Shape of the period:** ten minutes finishing Monday, then everyone downloads a photo, then Part 1
together off these slides, then they break out and edit in three stages: **Temperature first as a review
of yesterday, then Exposure and Contrast, then the four region sliders.**
**Part 1 is call-and-response.** Each prompt slide tells them to do one thing, look at their screen, and
report what they see. **Wait for answers.** The clipping demo only works if they are the ones who caused
it.
**Post a set of photos and let them pick one.** Choice keeps them invested, and because the lesson is
about reading their own graph rather than matching your result, different photos are fine.
**Two exports due:** `LastName_Tone_A_01` after Exposure and Contrast, `LastName_Tone_B_01` after the
four region sliders.
**Hold the line on which sliders are allowed**, in this order: **Temperature and Tint**, then **Exposure
and Contrast**, then **Highlights, Shadows, Whites, Blacks.** **No Saturation, Vibrance, Clarity or
HSL** today, or somebody finds Saturation and the photo becomes a different assignment.
**White balance first is not just review, it is the right order.** A color cast makes every tone decision
after it a guess, because a dark area and a blue area look the same.

> "Version B: add Highlights, Shadows, Whites, Blacks" with 7.9.6; "Export both" with 7.4.7. Keep both halves readable from the back of the room. Design it to stay
> on the board all period. One slide.

### Slide 2: Finish yesterday first

> Create one slide for a high school design class titled "Four Files. Yesterday's." Body: if your three

### Slide 3: Pick a photo

> Create one slide for a high school design class titled "Pick One. Download It." Three numbered steps in
> large text: "1. Open the photo set in Google Classroom and pick ONE you like. 2. Save it into YOUR OWN
> folder. Not the desktop. 3. Open it in Camera Raw." Then a box: "Different people will have different
> photos. That is fine. Today is about reading YOUR graph, not matching mine." One slide.

### Slide 4: What the histogram is

> Create one concept slide for a high school photography class titled "The Histogram." Draw a simple
> labeled histogram diagram: a horizontal axis with "DARK" labeled at the far left and "BRIGHT" at the
> far right, and a mound of data sitting across the middle. Add a vertical label on the left reading
> "HOW MUCH." Then only three short facts underneath, in large text: "LEFT is dark. RIGHT is bright." "The
> HEIGHT is how much of the photo is that bright." "That is the whole thing." Then a box: "Your eyes
> adjust to a bad photo within about ten seconds. The graph does not." One slide.

### Slide 5: Four shapes. Which one is yours?

> Create one slide for a high school photography class titled "Which One Is Your Photo?" Show FOUR small
> labeled histogram diagrams in a row or a two-by-two grid, each clearly different in shape. First,
> "DARK": a tall mound crowded against the left edge with the right two thirds empty. Second, "BRIGHT":
> a tall mound crowded against the right edge with the left two thirds empty. Third, "FLAT": a narrow
> low hump in the middle only, with obvious empty gaps at BOTH ends. Fourth, "USING THE RANGE": data
> spread across the whole width, reaching toward both ends, with no tall spike jammed against either
> wall. Under the fourth one add in large text: "THIS IS THE TARGET." Then a prompt box at the bottom
> headed "LOOK AT YOUR SCREEN": "Which of the four is yours? Say it out loud." One slide.

### Slide 6: What a gap means, and what a wall means

> Create one slide for a high school photography class titled "Gaps And Walls." Two labeled histogram
> diagrams side by side. Left, headed "A GAP AT THE END": data stopping well short of the right edge,
> with the empty space marked. Underneath: "You are not using the brightest part of the range. The photo
> looks washed out, dull, and a bit grey. This is FIXABLE." Right, headed "A SPIKE AGAINST THE WALL":
> data running up hard against the right edge in a tall vertical bar. Underneath: "Everything in that
> area became the same pure white. There is nothing left to tell apart. This is CLIPPING." Then a box in
> very large text: "A gap is a wasted opportunity. A wall is lost information." One slide.

### Slide 7: Turn on the warnings

> Create one slide for a high school photography class titled "Do This Before Anything Else." Body: find
> the two small triangles in the top corners of the histogram, and click both. Then what they do, in
> large text: "BLUE overlay on the photo = shadow detail is GONE." and "RED overlay = highlight detail is
> GONE." Then a prompt box headed "LOOK AT YOUR SCREEN, EVERY SLIDER STILL AT ZERO": "Is there any red or
> blue on your photo already? Where?" Then a smaller line: "Leave these on for the rest of the week." One
> slide.

### Slide 8: Everybody push Exposure to +2

> Create one task slide for a high school photography class titled "Break It On Purpose." One instruction
> in very large text: "Drag EXPOSURE up to about +2.00. Now look at your photo." Then a prompt box headed
> "TELL ME": "What appeared? Where on the photo is it?" Then, in smaller text below, a line for after
> they answer: "Those red areas are now pure white. Not bright. Not light. The SAME white, all of them,
> with nothing to tell apart." Do not put the recovery question on this slide. One slide.

### Slide 9: Now bring it back

> Create one task slide for a high school photography class titled "Now Put It Back." One instruction in
> very large text: "Bring Exposure back to 0." Then a prompt box headed "TELL ME": "Did the detail come
> back?" Then the answer in two clearly separated blocks. First, headed "RED THAT APPEARED WHEN YOU
> PUSHED": "Comes back. You had not thrown anything away, you were only asking the file to show you more
> than it has in that spot. Back off and it returns." Second, headed "RED THAT WAS THERE AT ZERO": "Does
> NOT come back. The camera already lost it when the photo was taken. No slider recovers that." Then a box
> in very large text: "And once you EXPORT a clipped JPEG, it is gone for everybody, forever." One slide.

### Slide 10: Same thing at the dark end

> Create one task slide for a high school photography class titled "The Other End." One instruction in
> very large text: "Pull BLACKS down to −100." Then a prompt box headed "TELL ME": "What color appeared,
> and where?" Then underneath: "Those areas are now pure black. A shadow with something in it became a
> shadow with nothing in it." Then a line: "Put Blacks back to 0." Then a box: "This is why we avoid
> clipping at BOTH ends. The middle of the graph is detail. The walls are nothing." One slide.

### Slide 11: Exposure and Contrast, on the graph

> Create one slide for a high school photography class titled "What These Two Actually Do." Two labeled
> pairs of small histogram diagrams, before and after. First pair, headed "EXPOSURE": show the same mound
> of data sliding bodily to the right, with an arrow, and the caption "Slides the WHOLE graph. Everything
> moves together." Second pair, headed "CONTRAST": show a mound stretching outward toward both ends with
> arrows pointing away from the center, and the caption "Pulls the ends APART. The middle empties out and
> both ends fill up." Then a box in large text: "Which is why too much Contrast clips BOTH ends at once."
> One slide.

### Slide 12: The four that grab one part

> Create one slide for a high school photography class titled "One Slider Per Region." Draw a single wide
> histogram diagram with four labeled brackets underneath pointing at different parts of it, left to
> right: "BLACKS: the far left END. where black begins"; "SHADOWS: the left SIDE, without moving the
> end"; "HIGHLIGHTS: the right SIDE, without moving the end"; "WHITES: the far right END. where white
> begins." Add a note in the middle of the diagram: "EXPOSURE lives here. Camera Raw has no separate
> midtone slider." Then a box in large text: "Blacks and Whites move the ENDS. Shadows and Highlights
> move what is sitting INSIDE them." One slide.

### Slide 13: The target

> Create one slide for a high school design class titled "What You Are Aiming For." A numbered checklist
> in large text, six items: "1. White things look white. No color cast. 2. The graph reaches toward both
> ends. 3. No tall spike jammed against either wall. 4. No red or blue, except a bulb, a window, or sun
> off chrome. 5. The subject sits in the middle, not at an edge. 6. Toggle the edits off and on, and the
> difference is obvious." Then a box in very large text: "Check your photo against this list BEFORE you
> export. Both times." One slide.

### Slide 14: White balance first

> Create one slide for a high school photography class titled "Start With Yesterday." One idea in very
> large text: "White balance comes FIRST. Always." Then the reason, in two short lines: "A color cast
> makes every tone decision after it a guess." and "You cannot tell a dark area from a blue one." Then
> three numbered steps: "1. Find something in the photo you KNOW should be white or grey. 2. Fix the cast
> with TEMPERATURE. Use Tint if you need it. 3. Write down the Kelvin you landed on, and which way you had
> to move it." Then a box: "Nothing in there yet that you know is white? Say so on your sheet and go by
> what looks right." One slide.

### Slide 15: Version A

> Create one task slide for a high school photography class titled "Version A: Two Sliders." In very
> large text: "EXPOSURE and CONTRAST. Leave Temperature where you set it." Then three numbered tasks: "1.
> Get as close to the six goals as you can. 2. Write down your Exposure and your Contrast. 3. Go through the goals list.
> Which ones can you tick, and which one is STILL OFF? Name it, and say where in the photo." Then a box:
> "Then export: Save Image, JPEG, LastName_Tone_A_01. Upload it." One slide.

### Slide 16: Version B

> Create one task slide for a high school photography class titled "Version B: Add Four More." In large
> text: "Highlights, Shadows, Whites and Blacks are now allowed. LEAVE Exposure and Contrast where they
> are." Then three numbered tasks: "1. Fix the thing you named at the end of Version A. 2. Go through the
> goals list one more time. 3. In one sentence: what were you able to do that Exposure and Contrast could
> not?" Then a box: "Then export: Save Image, JPEG, LastName_Tone_B_01. Upload it." Then a final line in
> large text: "Still no Saturation, no Vibrance, no Clarity, no HSL today. That is the rest of the week."
> One slide.

## 2. Video & Sound: Ten minutes, then build

**Documents:** `week-7/project.md`, `week-7/handouts/cut-plan-and-storyboard.md`,
`week-8/handouts/layering-and-audio.md`
**The gate still holds.** Anyone without a storyboard finishes the storyboard. **No CapCut.** Check it
individually, as you did Monday, and do not soften it on day two.
**Run it as:** ten minutes at the front, then work time for the rest of the period.
**The ten minutes is worth it** because day one of an edit is where people sink the whole period into
three perfect seconds. Rough assembly first is the fix.
**Circulate for one thing:** is their timeline in the order of their storyboard? If a student is
color-correcting shot 2 before shot 8 exists, stop them.

### Slide 1: Standards and agenda

> Create ONE slide for a high school video production class that holds both the standards and the agenda.
> Title: "Today." Top half, headed "What we are covering," lists these with their FULL text, not just the
> numbers: 7.3.7 Edit video footage. 7.7.5 Select a linear or nonlinear editing system and edit the
> video. 2.9.5 Develop a timeline, a communication plan, a task breakdown, costs (e.g., equipment,
> labor), deliverables, and responsibilities for completion. Bottom half,
> headed "What we are doing," is a short two-column list pairing each activity with its competency:
> "Storyboard check. No storyboard, no CapCut" with 2.9.5; "Rough assembly: the whole thing, badly, end
> to end" with 7.3.7; "Then refine, in order" with 7.7.5. Keep both halves readable from the back of the
> room. One slide.

### Slide 2: Rough assembly first

> Create one slide for a high school video editing class titled "Build It Badly First." Explain the rough
> assembly: put every shot on the timeline in story order, roughly trimmed, before you make anything good.
> Then in very large text: "The whole thing, badly, end to end. THEN refine." Below it, a short list
> headed "Why": you find out your edit is two minutes short while you can still fix it; you stop
> polishing a shot you are going to cut; you can watch it and see what is actually wrong. Then a box:
> "The classic day-one mistake is spending the whole period making the first eight seconds perfect. Do not
> be that person." One slide.

### Slide 3: Housekeeping that will save you

> Create one slide for a high school video editing class titled "Two Things, Or You Lose Your Work." Two
> numbered items in large text. One: "Name your project NOW, with your own name in it. Not 'Untitled'."
> Two: "Do NOT move, rename, or delete your footage folder after you import it. The editor is only
> pointing at those files. Move them and your timeline goes to grey boxes." Then a box at the bottom: "If
> you see grey boxes or 'media offline,' the files moved. Put them back where they were." One slide.

## 3. Aviation UAS: Settle Section 1, then the system moving through

**Documents:** `week-8/handouts/weather-brief-project.md` (Section 1 review, then Section 2),
**`week-8/teacher-notes.md` (the run of show and the Section 1 answer guide, read it first)**
**The shape of the day: first third is Monday's answers, out loud, together. Second third is new content.
The last half of the period is theirs to work.** Monday was all front-of-room and they left with nothing
finished.
**The re-teach is inside the review, not next to it.** Questions 3, 6 and 7 of Section 1 *are* why air
moves and density altitude. Go slowly on those three and quickly on 1, 4 and 5.
**Before class:** today's METAR, TAF and a current surface map on screen. **They stay up through work
time.**
**The four fronts are predict-then-reveal.** They commit a guess in writing before they see anything.
**Collect Q18 at the door.**

### Slide 1: Standards and agenda

> Create ONE slide for a high school aviation and drone class that holds both the competencies and the
> agenda. Title: "Today: The System Moving Through." Top half, headed "What we are covering," lists these
> with their FULL text, not just the numbers: 7.6.3 Explain the effects of temperature on weather. 7.6.9
> Describe weather system formation, including air masses and fronts. 7.6.6 Identify wind patterns based
> on weather systems. Bottom half, headed "What we are doing," is a short two-column list pairing each
> activity with its competency: "Section 1 answers, out loud, together" with 7.6.3; "Where our air came
> from" with 7.6.9; "The four fronts: you predict, then we find out" with 7.6.9; "Lows and highs, and
> which one is a flyable day" with 7.6.6; "Then HALF THE PERIOD to finish Section 2" with 7.6.9. Keep both
> halves readable from the back. Design it to stay on the board all period. One slide.

### Slide 2: How we are doing this today

> Create one slide for a high school class titled "Two Rules For Today." First, headed "TURN AND TALK,"
> three numbered steps in large text: "1. THIRTY SECONDS alone. Write something down. No talking. 2. SIXTY
> SECONDS with your partner. Both of you say your answer out loud. 3. I call on two people BY NAME. Not
> hands." With a box under it: "When I call on you, I might ask for YOUR PARTNER'S answer. That is why you
> both say it out loud." Second, headed "THE LAST HALF OF THE PERIOD IS YOURS": "Section 2 gets finished
> in here today, not at home. Which means the first half moves." One slide.

### Slide 3: Section 1, out loud

> Create one slide for a high school aviation class titled "Section 1: Let's Settle It." Body: we are going
> through yesterday's questions together, out loud. Fix your own answers as we go. Then a simple numbered
> list of what is coming, short: "1 and 2. Your METAR, and how close temperature and dew point are. 3.
> What wind actually is. 4 and 5. Which surfaces heat fastest, and where the bumpy air is. 6 and 7. Is
> today's air thick or thin, and what that costs you. 8. Your verdict." Then a box in large text: "Three
> of these are the ones that did not land yesterday. We are going to stop on those." One slide.

### Slide 4: Settle it: why air moves

> Create one very simple slide for a high school aviation class titled "Question 3: What Is Wind?" Only
> three short lines, large: "The ground heats unevenly." "Warm air is less dense, so it rises." "Something
> has to take its place." Then, in the largest text on the slide: "That taking-its-place IS wind." Then a
> question box headed "TURN AND TALK": "The asphalt is 130 degrees. The grass beside it is 85. The air
> over the asphalt goes up. What takes its place, and where did it come from?" Then smaller, headed "If you
> finish early": "So which direction is the wind blowing at the edge of the lot?" Nothing else on the
> slide. One slide.

### Slide 5: Settle it: density altitude

> Create one very simple slide for a high school drone class titled "Questions 6 and 7: Density Altitude."
> One definition only, in very large text: "How THIN the air is behaving. No matter where you are
> standing." Then three short words stacked: "Heat. Humidity. Altitude." with the line "All three make it
> thinner." Then a question box headed "TURN AND TALK": "Same drone, same battery, same pilot. One flight
> on a 40-degree morning, one on a 95-degree humid afternoon. Which one gives you fewer minutes in the air,
> and WHY?" Then a box at the bottom in large text: "Last week a payload ate your margin. You CHOSE the
> payload. You did not choose the weather." Nothing else on the slide. One slide.

### Slide 6: Where our air came from

> Create one simple slide for a high school aviation class titled "Air Masses." One idea in large text: "A
> big body of air takes on the character of the ground it sat over." Then four short examples in a plain
> list: sat over the Gulf, so warm and wet; sat over Canada, so cold and dry; sat over the Pacific, so cool
> and damp; sat over the desert southwest, so hot and dry. Then a question box headed "TURN AND TALK": "Air
> that spent three days over the Gulf of Mexico, versus air that spent three days over Canada. What is
> different about it when it gets to Ohio?" Then smaller, headed "If you finish early": "Which one of those
> would you rather fly in, and why?" One slide.

### Slide 7: The four fronts: you go first

> Create one slide for a high school aviation class titled "Predict First. Write It Down." Explain in one
> line at the top: "You already know that dense air sinks and less dense air rises. Everything below
> follows from that." Then four short numbered setups, each as a question with NO answer: "1. COLD FRONT.
> Cold, dense air arrives at a wall of warm air. Which air goes up? Gentle or steep? Fast or slow?" "2.
> WARM FRONT. Warm, light air arrives behind cold air already sitting on the ground. Can it push that cold
> air out of the way?" "3. STATIONARY FRONT. Neither one is winning. What does your week look like?" "4.
> OCCLUDED FRONT. A cold front catches up to a warm front. Where does the warm air go?" Then a box in very
> large text: "Write a guess in all four rows on your sheet BEFORE the next slide. A wrong guess you wrote
