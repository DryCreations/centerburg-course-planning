## 1. Design Techniques: Photography opens

**Documents:** `week-7/handouts/photo-worksheet.md` (**digital, make-a-copy**, three days),
`week-7/handouts/photo-worksheet-key.md` (teacher only),
`week-7/handouts/photo-composition.md` (still useful, composition reference)

**Cameras stay in the room**, shared, signed out per period. All four bodies shoot RAW, T3 included,

**Run it as:** BPA for about ten minutes, shutter priority on the board for fifteen, then shoot.
**While waiting for a camera, students answer written questions.** Say that up front.

### Slide 4: Shutter speed

> Create one concept slide for a high school photography class titled "Shutter Speed." Define it: how
> long the sensor is exposed to light, written as a fraction of a second. Show a compact scale in large
> monospace text from 1/1000 down to 1 second, labeled FROZEN at the fast end and BLURRED at the slow
> end. Add in large text: "A bigger bottom number is a FASTER shutter. 1/1000 is much faster than 1/60."
> Then a box headed "The trade nobody warns you about": the shutter is also how long light gets in, so a
> faster shutter means a DARKER photo; indoors past about 1/250 you will start getting very dark images.
> One slide.

### Slide 5: Today's three challenges

> Create one assignment slide for a high school photography class titled "Mode Dial: Tv." Subtitle: "You
> pick the shutter. The camera picks the aperture." Then three numbered challenges, each with a RANGE to
> try, not a single number: "1. FREEZE IT. Something moving, frozen sharp. Try 1/250, 1/500, 1/1000. 2.
> BLUR IT ON PURPOSE. Same subject, visible motion blur. Try 1/30, 1/15, 1/8, half a second. Brace the
> camera. 3. THE PANNING SHOT. Move WITH the subject so they stay sharp and the background streaks. Try
> 1/30, 1/15, 1/8." Add a box in large text: "Try the whole range. Finding the edge yourself IS the
> assignment." One slide.

### Slide 6: How to pan

> Create one technique slide for a high school photography class titled "The Panning Shot." Numbered in
> large text: "1. Stand still, feet planted, turn from the WAIST. 2. Start tracking them BEFORE you press
> the shutter. 3. Keep turning through the press and after it. Stopping is what ruins it. 4. Smooth,
> steady, matched to their walking pace." Then a box: "Someone walking is SLOW. To blur the background
> behind a walker you need a genuinely slow shutter. Start at 1/30." Then in large text: "Expect to shoot
> fifteen and keep one. That is the normal ratio for this shot, for everybody." One slide.

## 2. Video & Sound: J-cuts and L-cuts

**Documents:** `week-7/handouts/split-edits-lab.md` (student-facing, post it)
**Before class:** download the Cinestudy hospital scene clip pack and put it in the shared folder.
**Verify the download works and check the file format**, and grab the lower-resolution version if the
machines struggle with UltraHD.
`cinestudy.org/2025/03/12/interactive-project-l-cuts-split-edits/`
**Run it as:** board lecture on cut types, then they edit the supplied scene. Everyone gets the same
clips, so the comparison afterward is entirely about decisions.
**Why supplied footage:** their own material is not conversations, and split edits live in conversations.

### Slide 1: Standards today

> Create one standards slide for a high school video production class. Title: "Today's Standards." List
> these with their FULL text, not just the numbers, in large readable type: 7.3.7 Edit video footage.
> 7.7.6 Add transitions (dissolves, wipes, cuts), titles, special effects, and digital effects. 7.7.5
> Select a linear or nonlinear editing system and edit the video. 7.8.8 Perform audio mixing. Design it
> to stay readable on the board all period. One slide.

### Slide 2: Today, tied to those standards

> Create one agenda slide for a high school video production class titled "Today." A table with two
> columns, WHAT WE ARE DOING and WHICH STANDARD. Rows: "Learn the cut types and what each is for" /
> 7.7.6. "Edit a supplied conversation scene using split edits" / 7.3.7 and 7.7.5. "Separate picture from
> sound and offset them" / 7.8.8 and 7.7.6. Then a line at the bottom: "We're using supplied footage
> today. Split edits live in conversations, and your footage isn't conversations. Everyone gets the same
> clips, so the difference between our versions is entirely decisions." One slide.

### Slide 3: The three shapes

> Create one diagram slide for a high school video production class titled "Three Ways To Cut." Show
> three stacked timeline diagrams in monospace text, each with a PICTURE row and an AUDIO row. First,
> STRAIGHT CUT, both rows changing at the same point. Second, J-CUT, the audio row changing EARLIER than
> the picture row, labeled "you hear the new person before you see them." Third, L-CUT, the audio row
> changing LATER than the picture row, labeled "you cut to a listener while the speaker keeps talking."
> Add a line at the bottom: "Look at the shapes. That's where the letters come from." One slide.

### Slide 4: Why it matters

> Create one slide for a high school video production class. In very large text: "This is why
> professional dialogue feels smooth and yours hasn't." Below, smaller: "It isn't the camera and it isn't
> the microphone. It's that almost nobody cuts picture and sound at the same frame, and until today you
> didn't know that was a choice you were allowed to make." Then a short line: "Cutting picture and sound
> together on every line makes a conversation feel like a tennis match." One slide.

### Slide 5: The lab

> Create one assignment slide for a high school video production class titled "The Lab." Numbered: "1.
> Copy the clips into YOUR OWN project folder. 2. Watch every clip once before you cut anything. 3. Build
> a STRAIGHT CUT version first. Watch how mechanical it feels. That's what you're about to fix. 4. Unlink
> the audio from the video. This is the one mechanical skill today. 5. Build three J-cuts. 6. Build three
> L-cuts. 7. Compare the two versions." Then a box: "Adjust by TENTHS of a second, not seconds. Half a
> second is usually enough." Add a line: "Turn in BOTH versions, plus a log: timecode, J or L, and how
> much offset." One slide.

## 4. Middle School CS: One more thing with lists

**Your plan.** Thursday and Friday are work days.

### Slide 1: Today

> Create one work slide for a middle school computer science class titled "One More Thing With Lists."
> Leave a large open area where the teacher pastes the day's steps. Add a box at the bottom with the
> four things that go wrong: getting the wrong item, always off by one, you used 1 for the first item and
> the first is 0; crashes partway through, you went to length instead of length minus 1; only one item
> ever comes out, you used a fixed number where the index belongs; nothing happens, the list is empty.
> One slide.

### Board version

```
ONE MORE THING WITH LISTS

REMEMBER
  the index isn't WHICH ONE. it's HOW FAR FROM THE START.
  first item = 0
  for index from 0 to (length of list) - 1

IF IT BREAKS
  always off by one  -> you used 1 for the first item
  crashes partway    -> you went to length, not length - 1
  only one item      -> fixed number where index belongs
  nothing happens    -> the list is empty

THU + FRI: work days.
```
