## 1. Design Techniques: Journey, then wireframe

**Run it as:** nobody opens Figma today. The wireframe has to exist on paper first.

### Slide 1: As You Come In

> Create one "As You Come In" slide for a high school design class. Big title: "As You Come In." Body: "Get
> out yesterday's marked-up screenshot and a fresh sheet of paper. Still no computers. Today you find exactly
> where the screen breaks, then draw the fix." Large, clean. One slide.

### Slide 2: Today

> Create one agenda slide for a high school design class titled "Today: Map It, Then Draw It." Agenda: 1)
> Write what the person came to DO, 2) Number the steps and find the breakdown point, 3) Wireframe your fix on
> paper, 4) Partner test the wireframe. Add a box: "Figma is tomorrow. Paper is the cheapest place to be
> wrong." Large readable text. One slide.

### Slide 3: Your wireframe has one job

> Create one slide for a high school design class titled "Your Wireframe Has One Job." Body: "You found the
> step where the screen breaks. Your wireframe has to FIX that step. Before you draw anything, write at the
> top of the page: the one thing a person came here to do. Then make that the most obvious thing on the
> screen. Everything else gets smaller, moves down, or gets deleted." Add in large text: "Deleting is a
> design decision." One slide.

### Slide 4: What goes in a wireframe

> Create one slide for a high school design class titled "Boxes and Labels. That Is All." A checklist: a
> labeled box for every element; ONE clear primary action, bigger or bolder than everything else, and circle
> it; a deliberate reading order, number your boxes 1, 2, 3 in the order you want them read; grouping, related
> things close together and unrelated things apart. Add: "No color. No real images. No font choices. If you
> are picking a color you are doing the wrong step." One slide.

### Slide 5: Do Now

> Create one "Do Now" slide for a high school design class. Big title: "Do Now: Map, Then Draw." Numbered
> body: "1) Write the one thing a person came to this screen to do. 2) Number the steps they take and circle
> where it breaks. 3) Sketch your redesigned screen: boxes and labels only. 4) Number your boxes in reading
> order. 5) Circle your primary action. 6) Hand it to a partner WITHOUT saying what it is, and ask them what
> the screen is for and where they would tap first." Add a box: "If they guess wrong, your hierarchy is not
> working yet. Fix it before you build tomorrow." One slide.

## 2. Video & Sound: Scene breakdown

### Slide 1: As You Come In

> Create one "As You Come In" slide for a high school video class. Big title: "As You Come In." Body: "Get
> out your idea and yesterday's format drill. Warm-up question: how many different PLACES does your video
> happen in? Write the number down." Large, clean. One slide.

### Slide 2: Today

> Create one agenda slide for a high school video class titled "Today: Break It Into Scenes." Agenda: 1) What
> counts as a new scene, 2) Write your scene list, 3) Start drafting. Add a box: "A new scene heading means a
> new PLACE or a new TIME. Not a new camera angle." Large readable text. One slide.

### Slide 3: What counts as a new scene

> Create one slide for a high school video class titled "When Do You Need a New Scene Heading?" Two columns.
> Left, "YES, new heading": the location changes; the time changes, morning to night; you cut away to
> somewhere else entirely. Right, "NO, same scene": a different camera angle; a new character walks in; the
> conversation shifts; a close up instead of a wide. Caption: "Pick one name for each location and reuse it
> EXACTLY. HALLWAY, then HALL, then MAIN HALL reads as three different places to your crew." One slide.

### Slide 4: Do Now

> Create one "Do Now" slide for a high school video class. Big title: "Do Now: Scene List, Then Pages."
> Numbered body: "1) List every scene heading your video needs, in order, correctly formatted: INT. or EXT.,
> the location, the time. 2) Count them. Two or three scenes is normal for a 2 to 3 page script. 3) Next to
> each, write one line: what HAPPENS in this scene. 4) Then start writing page one." Add a box: "Only one
> scene? Your video may be one long shot, which is fine, or you may be missing a moment. Fifteen scenes? You
> are writing a feature. Cut." One slide.

## 3. Aviation UAS: Sky, temperature, and weather codes

### Slide 1: As You Come In

> Create one "As You Come In" slide for a high school drone class. Big title: "As You Come In." Body: "Pull
> today's METAR for our field. Decode the wind and the visibility out loud to the person next to you. Then
> look at the part you could not read yesterday, the letters and numbers in the middle. That is today."
> Large, clean. One slide.

### Slide 2: Sky condition

> Create one slide for a high school drone class titled "How Much Sky, and How High." Show a table. Left
> column the code, right column the coverage: SKC or CLR is clear; FEW is 1 to 2 eighths; SCT is scattered, 3
> to 4 eighths; BKN is broken, 5 to 7 eighths; OVC is overcast, all 8. Then in large text underneath: "The
> three digits after the code are the height in HUNDREDS of feet. OVC007 is 700 feet. SCT045 is 4,500 feet.
> ADD TWO ZEROS." Add a box: "BKN or OVC is a CEILING, the lowest layer you cannot see through." One slide.

### Slide 3: Temperature and dew point

> Create one slide for a high school drone class titled "24/13." Body: "Temperature over dew point, always in
> Celsius. A minus sign shows as M, so M02/M05 is minus 2 over minus 5." Then, in a box in large text: "When
> the two numbers get CLOSE, expect fog or mist. A METAR reading 18/17 is one degree apart and is telling you
> the air is nearly saturated. That is a visibility problem about to happen." One slide.

### Slide 4: Weather codes

> Create one slide for a high school drone class titled "The Letters in the Middle." A two column table: RA
> is rain; SN is snow; BR is mist; FG is fog; HZ is haze; TS is thunderstorm; DZ is drizzle. Then underneath:
> "Intensity goes in front. A minus is light, a plus is heavy, nothing means moderate. So -RA is light rain
> and +TSRA is a heavy thunderstorm with rain." Add a box: "VC means in the vicinity. VCTS is a thunderstorm
> nearby but not overhead. Treat that as a no-go anyway." One slide.

### Slide 5: Do Now

> Create one "Do Now" slide for a high school drone class. Big title: "Do Now: Decode Drill 2." Body: "Pull
> three live METARs. This time decode EVERY field: station, time in Zulu and local, wind, visibility, every
> cloud layer with its real height in feet, temperature and dew point, any weather codes, and the altimeter.
> For each one, write one sentence: what is the most important thing this METAR is telling a drone pilot?"
> Add a box: "Finished? Find a station right now reporting a ceiling below 1,000 feet, and work out what that
> would mean for us." One slide.

## 4. Middle School CS: `for index`

**Run it as:** trace the table out loud on the board **before** they build. The zero start catches everyone.

### Slide 1: As You Come In

> Create one "As You Come In" slide for a middle school computer science class. Big title: "As You Come In."
> Body: "Open Star Catcher and run it. Your ten stars should all appear at once in the same instant. That is
> correct, we fix it tomorrow. Today: making them line up." Large, clean, minimal. One slide.

### Slide 2: repeat cannot count

> Create one slide for a middle school computer science class titled "repeat Has a Problem." Body: "A repeat
> loop runs your code ten times, but it has no idea WHICH time it is on. Every pass is identical. So if you
> want ten stars in ten different places, repeat cannot help you. You need a loop that counts." One slide,
> large text.

### Slide 3: for index

> Create one slide for a middle school computer science class titled "for index: The Counting Loop." Show
> this code in a monospace box: "for index from 0 to 4:" then indented "set star to sprite of kind Food" and
> "set star position to x (20 + index * 30), y 0". Underneath, a trace table with columns Pass, index, and "x
> works out to": pass 1 index 0 gives 20; pass 2 index 1 gives 50; pass 3 index 2 gives 80; pass 4 index 3
> gives 110; pass 5 index 4 gives 140. Caption: "Five stars, evenly spaced, from one block." One slide, large
> readable text.

### Slide 4: Zero

> Create one slide for a middle school computer science class titled "It Starts at Zero." Body in very large
> text: "for index from 0 to 4 runs FIVE times. It counts 0, 1, 2, 3, 4." Underneath: "This catches
> absolutely everybody at least once. If you get one more star than you expected, this is why." One slide.

### Slide 5: Do Now

> Create one "Do Now" slide for a middle school computer science class. Big title: "Do Now: Line Them Up."
> Numbered body: "1) Replace your repeat with a 'for index from 0 to 4'. 2) Set the star's x to 20 plus index
> times 30. 3) Run it. You should get a row of evenly spaced stars. 4) Now change the 30 and see what
> happens. 5) Then try: set star vy to 30 plus index times 10, so every star in the row falls at a different
> speed." Add a box: "Finished? Make a row that goes DOWN the side instead of across the top. What do you
> change?" One slide.

## 5. Yearbook: Rotation and work

### Slide 1: Today (the only slide, leave it up)

> Create one work-time slide for a high school yearbook class. Big title: "Today." Two columns. Left, "On the
> camera list": shoot your shutter speed pair, same subject, one frozen at 1/500 or faster, one blurred at
> 1/30 or slower, then offload and name the files right away. Write down the actual shutter speed you used
> for each. Right, "Everyone else": spread work. Every photo frame gets a caption box, every text box uses a
> preset, your design-by credit goes on. Add a reminder box at the bottom: "In the gym? Shutter 1/500,
> aperture wide open, ISO up to 1600 or 3200. A grainy sharp photo beats a clean blurry one." And: "Due
> Friday: the pair AND your PDF check-in." One slide, clean, meant to stay on the board.
