## 1. Design Techniques: Changing one color

**Documents:** **`week-8/handouts/color-lab.md`** (today, post it with all three RAW files attached),
**`week-8/handouts/color-grading-extension.md`** (post as a **separate, ungraded assignment, due
Friday**)
**Three photos today**, and they edit all three. **The editing is the bulk of the period**, and the sheet
is only five questions.
**Slide 1 goes up before the bell** and carries the standards, the agenda, and the download steps. **They
need Finder open**, not the download bar: shift-click to select all three, right-click, Open With,
Photoshop. **Any version listed works.**
**First ten minutes they get all three to a decent base**, in order, with no questions attached. **Say
why the order matters:** a photo that is still too dark will not be saved by changing its greens.
**Then the color lesson**, which is three definitions and three jobs. **Question 1 is their notes from
that**, so do not rush it.
**Step 4 is the discovery that makes HSL click.** Drag one color's Saturation to −100 and **something
they expected to go grey will not**, because a sky is blue *and* aqua, grass is green *and* yellow, skin
is orange *and* red. One slider rarely owns a whole object.
**Then make one color win, on each of the three.** That is the assignment.
**Question 5 is the quiet one worth reading:** what did they go back and change after the color work?
Editing is not a one-way list, and that question is where they find out.
**Color grading is off the table today.** It is a separate, ungraded, due-Friday assignment for whoever
finishes. **We go deeper later in the year**, and saying that keeps anyone from thinking they missed it.

## 2. Video & Sound: Cutaways, and fixing your worst cut

**Documents:** `week-8/handouts/layering-and-audio.md` (questions 4 and 5), `week-7/project.md`
**Storyboards should all be done.** Anyone still planning is behind, not exempt. **Today is work time
with one demo at the front.**
**Demo it in CapCut yourself, on the projector, in under five minutes.** Three steps and they will have
it:
1. **Main clip stays on the bottom track, with its audio**
2. **Cutaway clip goes on the track above**, over the moment you want covered
3. **Mute the cutaway's audio.** The sound from underneath keeps running
**Then trim it to one or two seconds.** A long cutaway stops being a cover and becomes a scene.
**The question that matters is why the audio gets muted.** If both tracks play, you hear two rooms at
once and the illusion dies. That is question 5.
**Then send them to find their worst cut and cover it.** That is the actual assignment and it is
question 4.
**Circulate for one thing:** is the cutaway something a person in that scene could plausibly be looking
at? A random stock shot does not hide a cut, it announces one.

### Slide 1: Standards and agenda

> Create ONE slide for a high school video production class that holds both the standards and the agenda.
> Title: "Today." Top half, headed "What we are covering," lists these with their FULL text, not just the
> numbers: 7.3.7 Edit video footage. 7.7.6 Add transitions (dissolves, wipes, cuts), titles, special
> effects, and digital effects. 7.8.8 Perform audio mixing. Bottom half, headed "What we are doing," is a
> short two-column list pairing each activity with its competency: "Build a cutaway on the upper track"
> with 7.7.6; "Mute its audio so the scene underneath keeps running" with 7.8.8; "Find your worst cut and
> cover it" with 7.3.7; "Then keep building" with 7.3.7. One slide.

### Slide 2: A cutaway is three steps

> Create one diagram slide for a high school video editing class titled "A Cutaway Is Just A Clip On Top."
> Show a simple two-track timeline: a long clip on the bottom track labeled "main clip, KEEPS ITS AUDIO,"
> and a short clip sitting on the track above it labeled "cutaway, AUDIO MUTED, 1 to 2 seconds." Then
> three numbered steps beside it: "1. Main clip stays on the bottom track, with its audio. 2. Cutaway
> goes on the track ABOVE, over the moment you want covered. 3. MUTE the cutaway's audio." Then a box in
> large text: "Top covers bottom for picture. Audio from underneath keeps playing. The audience hears the
> scene continue while they look at something else." One slide.

### Slide 3: Why mute it

> Create one slide for a high school video editing class titled "Why The Audio Has To Go." Body: video
> tracks COVER each other, but audio tracks MIX. Then in very large text: "If you leave the cutaway's
> audio in, you hear two rooms at the same time." Then: "And the moment the audience hears a second room,
> they know they are being shown a trick. The cutaway stops hiding the cut and starts pointing at it."
> Then a box: "That is question 5, and it is the one worth the most." One slide.

### Slide 4: Now go find your worst cut

> Create one task slide for a high school video class titled "Find Your Worst Cut. Cover It." Body: play
> your edit through and watch for the moment that makes you wince, a jump, a stumble, a bad join. Then
> three numbered tasks: "1. Cover it with a cutaway on the upper track. 2. Trim the cutaway to one or two
> seconds. Longer and it stops being a cover and becomes a scene. 3. Watch it again. Did the bad cut
> disappear?" Then a box in very large text: "Use a cutaway something in that scene could plausibly be
> LOOKING AT. A random shot does not hide a cut, it announces one." Then a line: "Storyboards should be
> done. If yours is not, that is what you are doing today." One slide.

**One slide. Split down the middle.** Left is the pre-check, right is the goal. **It stays up while they

**They already wrote their limits yesterday**, Section 3 question 30. **Today those numbers get used**,

**A no-go is a real outcome.** If today's numbers exceed somebody's stated limits, the honest answer is
not to fly, and **that gets logged and graded as a correct call.** Say that before anyone goes outside.
**The centering requirement is new, and it applies to the orbit.** Keeping the target in the middle of
the frame means **yawing the aircraft as it circles**, not just flying a circle with the nose fixed. That
is a real step up from last week, so expect the first laps to wobble. `orbit-drill.md` is updated to make
it the standard rather than the bonus, with a scaffold for anyone drowning.
**The figure eight has no single target to center on**, so its standard stays what it was: even loops, a
clean crossover, constant altitude. **Say that out loud** so the figure-eight group does not think they
are being let off.
**Recording is the submission.** A student who thinks they have it films a lap, and the frame does not
lie about whether the target stayed centered the way your eyes from the ground might.
**Section 4 gets filled in after flying**, not during. The question that matters is what the brief
missed.

## 4. Middle School CS: Where is it?

**Documents:** `week-8/handouts/bell-ringer-thu.md` (the bell ringer),
`week-8/handouts/project-plan.md` (**due today**)
**Bell ringer: search a list.** Write a loop that finds the index of a value, and gives back **−1** when
it is not there.
**Yesterday you walked two lists together.** Today is one list and a question: is this in here, and
where?
**The −1 convention is worth two minutes.** You cannot return 0 for "not found," because 0 is a real
index: the first item. −1 is never a real index, so it is safe. **Every language does it this way**, and
now they know why.
**Then point them at the `index of` block** in the Arrays drawer. If their version has it, **they just
wrote it themselves**, which is a better way to meet a built-in than being handed it.
**Why it matters for their projects:** `remove value at index` needs an index, and until today they had
no way to find one. That is the payoff to say out loud.
**The plan is due today.** Walk the room and ask the one question that is the whole rubric: **what breaks
if I remove it?**

### Slide 1: Bell ringer

> Create one bell-ringer slide for a middle school computer science class titled "Where Is It?" Show in
> large monospace text: "set bag to array of  'rope'  'key'  'apple'  'map'". Then the task in large
> text: "Write a loop that finds WHERE 'key' is. It should give you back 1, because 'key' is at index 1."
> Then below it: "Then make it work when the thing is NOT there. Looking for 'sword' should give you back
> −1." Then a box: "Yesterday you walked two lists together. Today it is one list and a question: is this
> in here, and where?" One slide.

### Slide 2: Why minus one

> Create one slide for a middle school computer science class titled "Why −1?" Body: you cannot give back
> 0 to mean "not found." Then the reason in very large text: "0 is a REAL index. It is the first item."
> Then: "−1 is never a real index, so it is a safe way to say 'it is not in here.'" Then a box in large
> text: "Every programming language does it this way. Now you know why." Then a smaller line at the
> bottom: "Now look in the Arrays drawer for a block called 'index of'. If it is there, you just wrote it
> yourself." One slide.

### Slide 3: The plan is due

> Create one slide for a middle school computer science class titled "Plans Are Due Today." Body: bring
> me your plan and we go through it together. Then the list of six requirements in large text, each as a
> short line: "a LIST the game reads to make a decision," "a FOR or WHILE loop, not forever," "a
> CONDITIONAL that can go either way," "a VARIABLE that changes during play," "a way to WIN," "a way to
> LOSE." Then a box in very large text: "The question I will ask about each one: WHAT BREAKS IF I REMOVE
> IT? If the answer is 'nothing really,' that requirement is not met yet." Then a line: "Approved plan
> means you start building. Today." One slide.
