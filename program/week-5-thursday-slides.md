## 1. Design Techniques: Make it click, then test it

**Run it as:** the partner test is the real assessment. Watch for students narrating their design while the
partner uses it, and stop them.

### Slide 1: As You Come In

> Create one "As You Come In" slide for a high school design class. Big title: "As You Come In." Body: "Open
> your Figma file. Today: a second frame, connect them so it clicks, then a partner tries to use it without
> your help. Due tomorrow, so today is the day it needs to work." Large, clean. One slide.

### Slide 2: The steps

> Create one instructional slide for a high school design class titled "Make It Click." Large numbered list:
> 1) Press V, click the frame NAME above your screen, Ctrl+C then Ctrl+V. Drag the copy next to the original
> and rename it. 2) On the COPY, change whatever your button should change. 3) Top right, switch from Design
> to the PROTOTYPE tab. Click your button, drag the blue + across to the second frame. 4) Hit PRESENT and
> click it. Esc to come back. 5) Connect it back the other way. Add a box: "Nothing happens? You connected
> the wrong layer. Check the layers panel, select the actual button shape, drag again." One slide.

### Slide 3: The partner test

> Create one slide for a high school design class titled "The Partner Test." Body: "Hand your prototype to a
> partner and tell them ONE thing: the task. 'Order a coffee.' 'Find the game on Friday.' Then say nothing
> else." Then a list of what to watch: Where do they look first? Do they hesitate? Do they tap the wrong
> thing? Where do they ask a question? Add a box in large text: "You are not allowed to explain. Every time
> you want to explain something, that is a design problem you just found." One slide.

### Slide 4: Do Now

> Create one "Do Now" slide for a high school design class. Big title: "Do Now: Connect, Then Test." Body:
> "1) Build your second frame. 2) Connect both frames, both directions. 3) Test it in Present. 4) Find a
> partner, give them the task and nothing else, and watch. 5) Write down ONE thing you are changing because
> of what you saw." Add a box: "Tomorrow: the write-up and posting to your portfolio. Bring your teardown
> from Monday, you will need it." One slide.

## 2. Video & Sound: The table read

**Run it as:** insist on out loud. This is the highest-value day in the unit and it only works if they
actually speak the lines.

### Slide 1: As You Come In

> Create one "As You Come In" slide for a high school video class. Big title: "As You Come In." Body: "Have
> your draft open or printed. Find a partner. Today you read each other's scripts OUT LOUD, taking parts.
> Yes, really out loud. It is the only way to hear what does not work." Large, clean. One slide.

### Slide 2: Why out loud

> Create one slide for a high school video class titled "Why Out Loud." Body: "Dialogue that looks fine on a
> page often sounds nothing like a person. You cannot hear that by reading silently, because your brain
> smooths it over. Someone ELSE reading your line, cold, without knowing how you meant it, is the closest
> thing you get to an audience." One slide, large text.

### Slide 3: How to run it

> Create one slide for a high school video class titled "How a Table Read Works." Numbered: 1) Swap scripts.
> Each person takes a character, and one reads the action lines. 2) Read straight through without stopping,
> even when something is wrong. 3) The WRITER does not explain anything, and does not join in. Just listen
> and mark. 4) Mark every place the reader stumbled, paused wrong, or had to reread. 5) Then talk. Add a box:
> "A stumble is almost never the reader's fault. It is a line that is not written the way people talk." One
> slide.

### Slide 4: Do Now

> Create one "Do Now" slide for a high school video class. Big title: "Do Now: Read, Then Revise." Body: "1)
> Table read your partner's script, then yours. 2) Mark every stumble. 3) Revise: fix at least THREE things
> you heard. 4) Write down the partner's name and the three things you changed, you turn that in tomorrow."
> Add a box titled "Also check while you revise": is every action line something a camera could record? Are
> you between 2 and 3 pages? Does every scene heading use the same name for the same place? One slide.

## 3. Aviation UAS: TAFs, then your own limits

**Run it as:** the card is the point of the week. Every number needs a reason written next to it, and every
number should be stricter than Part 107.

### Slide 1: As You Come In

> Create one "As You Come In" slide for a high school drone class. Big title: "As You Come In." Body: "Pull
> our field's METAR and decode it silently, all the way. Then find the TAF for the same field. It looks
> similar but it is doing something different. What do you think it is?" Large, clean. One slide.

### Slide 2: TAF

> Create one slide for a high school drone class titled "METAR vs TAF." Two boxes side by side. Left, METAR:
> "What the weather IS doing, observed right now, at one moment." Right, TAF: "What the weather is EXPECTED to
> do, over a window of time." Underneath: "Same codes. The TAF uses FM to mark when something changes, so
> FM150200 means from 0200Z on the 15th, expect what follows." Add a box in large text: "The METAR tells you
> whether you can launch. The TAF tells you whether you will be able to finish." One slide.

### Slide 3: Personal minimums

> Create one slide for a high school drone class titled "Your Own Limits." Body: "Part 107 gives you the
> legal floor: 3 statute miles, 400 feet, cloud clearance. That is the absolute edge of what is allowed, not
> a target. Your PERSONAL MINIMUMS are the line you draw above that floor." Add in large text: "You decide
> them now, calm, at a desk. Not later, standing in a field with a drone in your hands and everybody waiting
> on you." One slide.

### Slide 4: The rule

> Create one slide for a high school drone class titled "The Rule That Makes It Worth Doing." Body in very
> large text: "If ANY single number is outside your limits, it is a no-go. Not a discussion. Not a vote. Not
> 'but we only need five minutes.'" Underneath: "You wrote these down when nothing was on the line. The
> version of you standing in a field with a class watching is not the one who should be renegotiating them."
> One slide.

### Slide 5: Do Now

> Create one "Do Now" slide for a high school drone class. Big title: "Do Now: Write Your Card." Body: "Fill
> in the Personal Minimums Card from Google Classroom. For every line: your number, and WHY you picked it.
> 'Twenty knots' is not an answer. 'Twenty knots, because the aircraft is rated near twenty-four and I want
> margin' is an answer. Sign it." Add a box: "Your numbers must be at least as strict as Part 107, and for
> most of them they should be stricter. You are a student, flying a school aircraft, near other people." And:
> "Also due: decode drill 4, one METAR and its TAF together." One slide.

## 4. Middle School CS: `while`, and the freeze

**Run it as:** deliberately write an infinite loop on the board and let them watch it hang. Then ask what is
missing.

### Slide 1: As You Come In

> Create one "As You Come In" slide for a middle school computer science class. Big title: "As You Come In."
> Body: "Open Star Catcher and play it for thirty seconds. Does it still run fast, or is it slowing down? If
> it is slowing down, something is not being destroyed. Find it first." Large, clean, minimal. One slide.

### Slide 2: while

> Create one slide for a middle school computer science class titled "while: Loop Until Something Changes."
> Body: "A while loop keeps going as long as a condition is true. It checks BEFORE every single pass." Show
> in a monospace box: "set timer to 30" then "while timer > 0:" indented "pause 1000" and "change timer by
> -1" then unindented "game over WIN". Caption: "Wait a second, take one off the timer, check again. When the
> timer hits zero, the condition is false and the loop stops." One slide, large readable text.

### Slide 3: The trap

> Create one slide for a middle school computer science class titled "How to Freeze Your Game." Show in a
> monospace box: "set timer to 30" then "while timer > 0:" indented "pause 1000". Then in large text
> underneath: "This one NEVER ends. Nothing inside it ever changes the timer, so 'timer > 0' is true forever
> and the game locks up." Add a box: "Something inside a while loop has to move it toward stopping. That is
> the rule." One slide.

### Slide 4: The four loops

> Create one slide for a middle school computer science class titled "All Four, Side by Side." A two column
> table. "repeat 10 times": use it when you know exactly how many times and you do not care which pass you
> are on. "for index from 0 to 9": when you know how many times AND you need the count, for spacing, speed,
> or color. "while (condition)": when you do not know how many times, only when to stop. "forever": when it
> should never stop while the game runs. One slide, clean table.

### Slide 5: Do Now

> Create one "Do Now" slide for a middle school computer science class. Big title: "Do Now: Pick One." Body:
> "Add ONE of these to your game: 1) A COUNTDOWN TIMER using a while loop that ends the game when it hits
> zero. 2) A DIFFICULTY RAMP where each wave falls faster than the last, using a variable that grows. 3)
> LEVELS: a 'repeat 3 times' around your wave, then a splash saying LEVEL UP." Add a box: "Your game has to
> END, win or lose. A game that runs forever with no ending is not finished." And: "Tomorrow: finish, then we
> play each other's." One slide.
