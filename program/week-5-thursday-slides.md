## 1. Design Techniques: The rest of the interactions

**Documents:** `handouts/interaction-labs.md`, `handouts/ui-patterns.md` (section 3)
**Run it as:** they have components and variants. Today is the remaining interaction types, taught as a set,
then work time. The recurring question is **where does this one live.**

### Slide 1: As You Come In

> Create one "As You Come In" slide for a high school design class. Big title: "As You Come In." Body: "Open
> Figma. Your nav bar should be a component by now, and your button should have variants. Warm-up question to
> think about: list every place in your app where tapping something should make something happen. How many are
> there?" Large, clean. One slide.

### Slide 2: Standards today

> Create one standards slide for a high school design class titled "Today's Standards." List these with their
> FULL text, not just the numbers, in large readable type: 2.15.1 Understand the UX/UI design process
> (vision, journey mapping, wireframing, prototyping) for the targeted platform. 7.1.2 Describe the components
> of interactive media. 7.2.10 Select and create visual design elements appropriate for the intended audience
> and use. Then a short line: "Today that means overlays, scrolling, selection, input, and feedback." Design it to stay readable on the board for the
> whole period. One slide.

### Slide 3: Four more interactions

> Create one slide for a high school design class titled "Four More." Four short blocks, each with what it is
> and when to use it. OVERLAY: something appears on top of a dimmed screen instead of replacing it. Use for
> confirmations and menus. Build it by duplicating a frame, covering it with a black rectangle at 40 percent
> opacity, and putting a smaller box on top. SCROLL: a list longer than the screen. Select the frame, turn on
> Clip content, then in the Prototype tab set Overflow scrolling to Vertical. SELECTION: picking one of
> several options and seeing which is picked. Now that you have variants, this is a Selected variant, not
> extra frames. INPUT: a field that looks typed in. An empty version with gray placeholder text, and a filled
> version with darker real text. One slide.

### Slide 4: Where does it live?

> Create one slide for a high school design class titled "Component or Screen?" Body in large text: "If it
> changes only that element, it goes IN THE COMPONENT. If it changes the screen, it goes ON THE SCREEN." Then
> a two column table. IN THE COMPONENT: a toggle flips; a heart fills; a button looks pressed; an option
> becomes selected; a field goes from empty to filled. ON THE SCREEN: tapping a row opens a detail page; a
> confirmation overlay appears; submitting shows a success screen; a back arrow returns you. Add a box: "Now
> that you have variants, most of the left column is a variant swap, not a new frame. That is the point of
> yesterday." One slide.

### Slide 5: Feedback, so it feels real

> Create one slide for a high school design class titled "Tell the Person It Worked." Four short blocks.
> SELECTED STATE: which option is picked right now, shown filled, colored, or check-marked. A choice with no
> visible selection is a choice a person cannot verify. MODAL: a box over a dimmed screen, dealt with before
> continuing. Use it to confirm something destructive, not to tell people things they did not ask. TOAST: a
> brief message that appears and disappears by itself. "Saved." "Added." LOADING STATE: a spinner or a gray
> outline of the content. Add a box in large text: "Nothing happening reads as broken. Even in a mockup,
> showing you thought about it counts." One slide.

### Slide 6: Do Now

> Create one "Do Now" slide for a high school design class. Big title: "Do Now: Build and Wire." Numbered: "1)
> Add at least TWO of today's four interactions to your app: overlay, scroll, selection, or input. 2) For each
> one, decide out loud whether it belongs in a component or on the screen, and build it there. 3) Add one
> piece of feedback somewhere: a selected state, a confirmation, or a toast. 4) Test everything in Present and
> click every path yourself." Add a box: "Tomorrow: the consistency pass and your first partner test.
> Something has to be clickable end to end by tomorrow, even if it is only two screens." One slide.

## 2. Video & Sound: Procedure, then film

**Documents:** `handouts/equipment-procedure.md`, `handouts/filming-day.md`, `handouts/review-packet.md`
**Run it as:** the procedure gets covered first, out loud, with the gear in front of them. Then filming,
same eight minute slots.
**First period did not get to shoot yesterday.** They get slots first today.

### Slide 1: The stake

> Create one slide for a high school video class titled "Next Week Depends on Today." Body: "Next week groups
> are supposed to spread out into the building: hallways, the gym, the commons, outside. That happens for
> groups that can prove they handle equipment correctly in a room where I can see them." Then in large text:
> "If a group cannot follow a schedule they wrote themselves, and cannot put a lens cap back on, they do not
> get a camera somewhere I am not standing." Add a box: "That is not a punishment. It is the same call any
> production company makes." One slide, direct and plain, not decorated.

### Slide 2: Standards today

> Create one standards slide for a high school video class titled "Today's Standards." List these with their
> FULL text, not just the numbers, in large readable type: 2.10 Operate equipment according to manufacturer specifications. 7.7.2 Analyze the script and storyboard to develop a production schedule. Then a short line: "Today that means
> the equipment procedure, then filming." Design it to stay readable on the board for the whole period. One slide.

### Slide 3: The three that have been missed

> Create one slide for a high school video class titled "Three Things. Every Time." A large numbered list with
> plenty of space: 1) THE LENS CAP GOES BACK ON. In your pocket while shooting, on the camera the moment you
> stop. Not on a table, not on a chair. 2) SIGN IT OUT AND SIGN IT BACK IN. Every time, even for eight
> minutes. The camera is yours from the moment you sign for it until I sign it back. 3) FOLLOW THE SCHEDULE
> YOU WROTE. If you are not in your slot, you are not on a camera. Add a box at the bottom: "Say these out
> loud with your group before you take a camera today." One slide, large and legible from across the room.

### Slide 4: Packing up starts before you think

> Create one slide for a high school video class titled "Five Minutes Left, You Start Packing." Body:
> "Returning gear correctly takes about five minutes. If you start when the bell is about to ring, you skip
> steps, and skipping steps is exactly what we are fixing." Then the return checklist as a numbered list: lens
> cap back on; power off; card offloaded, then back in the camera; battery out and on the charger; camera in
> its case, lens down, closed; tripod collapsed and put away; signed back in, and tell me anything that went
> wrong; table cleared of caps, cards, and cables. Add a box in large text: "Not done until I have seen it.
> Hand it back. Do not leave it on a table and walk away." One slide.

### Slide 5: Do Now

> Create one "Do Now" slide for a high school video class. Big title: "Do Now." Two columns. Left, "IN YOUR
> SLOT": say the three things out loud with your group; sign it out and check it in front of me; set up BEFORE
> your slot, not during; shoot ten seconds and play it back; work your call sheet; room tone; start packing
> with five minutes left. Right, "NOT IN YOUR SLOT": reopen your worksheet on Classroom and pick up where you
> stopped. Silently, people are recording. Turn it in at the bell either way. Add a box across the bottom:
> "Goal by the end of today: your footage is on a computer, named, and your shot log says what is done and
> what still is not." One slide, designed to stay on the board.

**Run it as:** almost no instruction. They have the guided example from yesterday. Today they do the three

### Slide 1: As You Come In

> Create one "As You Come In" slide for a high school drone class. Big title: "As You Come In." Body: "Open
> the sectional viewer and open your worksheet. Find yesterday's guided example and look at it again for a
> minute, so you remember what the finished answer looked like. Then you are doing three more on your own."
> Large, clean. One slide.

### Slide 2: Standards today

> Create one standards slide for a high school drone class titled "Today's Standards." List these with their
> FULL text, not just the numbers, in large readable type: 7.13 Evaluate the flight environment, including airspace classification and obstructions. Then a short line: "Today that means
> finding three points where airspace overlaps, and listing every layer with its floor and ceiling." Design it to stay readable on the board for the whole period. One slide.

### Slide 3: What a finished one looks like

> Create one slide for a high school drone class titled "One Point, Every Layer." Body: "Pick a point on the
> chart. Then work upward and list EVERY piece of airspace stacked over that exact spot, with the floor and
> the ceiling of each." Then a worked example laid out as a small table with columns Layer, Floor, and
> Ceiling, showing something like: Class G, surface, up to 700 feet; Class E, 700 feet, up to 1,200 feet;
> Class C shelf, 1,200 feet, up to 4,000 feet. Add a box in large text: "Your three points must have
> OVERLAPPING CIRCLES. A spot with one ring over it is not the exercise." One slide.

### Slide 4: Do Now

> Create one "Do Now" slide for a high school drone class. Big title: "Do Now: Find Three." Numbered: "1) Find
> three different points on the chart where circles OVERLAP. 2) For each one, list every airspace layer over
> that point, with its floor and its ceiling. 3) Say how you knew: which color, which line style, which
> numbers told you. 4) For each point, answer: could I fly a drone here, and what would I need?" Add a box
> titled "Stuck?": "Zoom out and look for airports close enough together that their rings cross. The legend is
> open in another tab. Nobody memorizes a sectional, you look it up." One slide.

## 4. Middle School CS: Practice, then build

**Documents:** `handouts/loop-practice-problems.md`
**Run it as:** problems on the board to open, then the rest of the period building. **No new content today.**

### Slide 1: As You Come In

> Create one "As You Come In" slide for a middle school computer science class. Big title: "As You Come In."
> Body: "Open Star Catcher and run it. Get out paper for some problems. No new blocks today. We are going back
> over what we already did, then you get most of the period to build." Large, clean, minimal. One slide.

### Slide 2: Problems, round 1

> Create one bell-ringer slide for a middle school computer science class titled "How Many? Where?" Two
> numbered problems in monospace boxes. Problem 1: "for index from 0 to 5: change score by 1" with the
> question "How many times does score change? Starting from 0, what is score at the end?" Problem 2: "for
> index from 0 to 3: set star x to (10 + index * 20)" with the question "List every x value." One slide, large
> readable text.

*(Answers: 1) Six times, score is 6. It counts 0,1,2,3,4,5, always one more than the last number. 2) 10, 30,
50, 70.)*

### Slide 3: Problems, round 2

> Create one bell-ringer slide for a middle school computer science class titled "Remainders and Nesting."
> Three numbered problems. 1) What is remainder of 9 ÷ 4? Remainder of 20 ÷ 5? Remainder of 7 ÷ 10? 2) Write
> the condition for a bonus every 6th point. 3) In a monospace box: "repeat 3 times:" indented "for index from
> 0 to 4:" indented further "spawn a star" with the question "How many stars total?" One slide, large readable
> text.

*(Answers: 1) 1, 0, and 7. That last one surprises them: if the first number is smaller, the remainder is the
whole thing. 2) `remainder of score ÷ 6 = 0`, not `= 6`. 3) 15. Inner times outer, not added.)*

### Slide 4: Do Now

> Create one "Do Now" slide for a middle school computer science class. Big title: "Do Now: Build." Body:
> "Most of today is work time. Get your game to a place where it actually plays." Then a checklist: a player
> that moves; stars spawned with a loop, not by hand; a row placed with for index, using the count for
> something; catching adds score and destroys the star; missing costs a life and destroys the star; it stays
> fast after a minute. Add a box: "Stuck on one thing for more than five minutes? Raise your hand. That is
> what I am here for." One slide.

### Board version (no projector)

```
1. for index from 0 to 5: change score by 1
   how many times? score at the end?        -> 6 times, score 6
                                               counts 0,1,2,3,4,5

2. for index from 0 to 3:
      set star x to (10 + index * 20)       -> 10, 30, 50, 70
   list every x

3. remainder of  9 / 4 = ?   -> 1
   remainder of 20 / 5 = ?   -> 0
   remainder of  7 / 10 = ?  -> 7  (smaller first number =
                                    the whole thing is left)

4. bonus every 6th point?    -> remainder of score / 6 = 0
                                NOT = 6

5. repeat 3 times:
      for index from 0 to 4:
         spawn a star
   how many stars?           -> 15   (inner TIMES outer)
```
