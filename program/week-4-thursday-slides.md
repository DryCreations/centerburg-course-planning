## 1. Design Techniques: Pick a broken screen and tear it apart

**Today's documents:** `courses/design-techniques/planning/week-4/handouts/thu-teardown-and-setup.md`,
`vocab.md`, `project.md`
**Post:** the UX/UI half of the vocab, and the teardown sheet.
**Go over:** UX vs UI, pick a redesign target, tear it down on paper. **Figma setup is the last 10 minutes
only.** **New project starts today, runs into next week.**

### Slide 1: As You Come In

> Create one "As You Come In" slide for a high school design class. Big title: "As You Come In." Body: "Think
> of an app or website that has genuinely annoyed you. Open it on your screen and find the exact page that
> annoyed you. Get a blank sheet of paper. Warm-up question: what specifically made it hard to use?" Large,
> clean. One slide.

### Slide 2: Standards + Today

> Create one standards and agenda slide for a high school design class titled "Today: Find Something Broken."
> List this standard with its full text: 2.15.1 Understand the UX/UI design process (vision, journey mapping,
> wireframing, prototyping) for the targeted platform. Then a short agenda: 1) UX vs UI, 2) Pick the screen
> you are going to redesign, 3) Tear it apart on paper, 4) Set up Figma in the last ten minutes. Add a box:
> "New project. It runs into next week." Large readable text. One slide.

### Slide 3: The project

> Create one slide for a high school design class titled "The Redesign Project." Body: "You are going to find
> a real screen that does not work, prove exactly what is wrong with it in design language, and redesign it so
> it works. ONE screen, phone sized. Not a whole app. This ends up on your portfolio as a before and after,
> which is the strongest thing you can show anyone: a real problem and your solution." One slide, large text.

### Slide 4: UX vs UI

> Create one slide for a high school design class titled "UX vs UI." Two large definitions side by side.
> Left: "UX, user experience: how it FEELS to use something. Is it clear? Can I find what I need?" Right:
> "UI, user interface: the actual screens, buttons, menus, and visuals you interact with." Underneath: "Good
> UI with bad UX is a beautiful screen nobody can use. You are fixing both." One slide.

### Slide 5: Picking your target

> Create one slide for a high school design class titled "Pick Your Screen." Left side, a checklist titled
> "It has to pass all three": you can screenshot it; it is school appropriate; something about it actually
> bothers you and you can say what in one sentence. Right side, a box titled "Stuck? Any of these": a food
> ordering app, a school or district page, an athletics schedule, a streaming service browse screen, a store
> checkout, a game's settings menu, a ticket or airline page, a recipe site buried in ads. Add at the bottom:
> "Get it approved before you mark it up." One slide, two column layout.

### Slide 6: "It's ugly" is not a finding

> Create one slide for a high school design class titled "Say What Is Actually Wrong." Show a two column
> table. Left column header "Instead of," right column header "Write." Rows: "It's cluttered" becomes "Nine
> things compete for attention with no clear first read"; "It's ugly" becomes "Three unrelated type families
> with no reason"; "Hard to read" becomes "Light gray text on white fails contrast at that size";
> "Confusing" becomes "The primary action looks identical to four secondary buttons." Caption underneath:
> "Use your Week 3 words: hierarchy, contrast, alignment, proximity, whitespace, reading order." One slide.

### Slide 7: The user journey

> Create one slide for a high school design class titled "Where Does It Break?" Body: "Every screen exists so
> somebody can DO something. Write down what they came to do. Then number the steps they take to get it. Then
> circle the exact step where it falls apart. That circled step is what your redesign has to fix." One slide,
> large text.

### Slide 8: Do Now

> Create one "Do Now" slide for a high school design class. Big title: "Do Now: Tear It Down." Numbered
> body: "1) Screenshot your screen. 2) Write one sentence: what somebody came to this screen to DO. 3) Number
> the steps and circle where it breaks. 4) Draw arrows to at least FIVE problems, using design words, not
> feelings. 5) Squint at it: is the first thing you see the thing they came for?" Large, clean. One slide.

### Slide 9: Turn and Talk

> Create one "Turn and Talk" slide for a high school design class. Big title: "Turn and Talk: What Is This
> For?" Body: "Hand your marked-up screenshot to a partner without saying anything about it. They tell YOU:
> what do you think this screen is for, and what is the first thing it wants you to do? If they guess wrong,
> that is not their fault, that is your evidence." One slide, large text.

### Slide 10: Figma setup (last 10 minutes)

> Create one slide for a high school design class titled "Set Up Figma, 10 Minutes." Numbered list: 1) Go to
> figma.com. 2) Log in, Continue with Google, use your SCHOOL account. 3) SKIP the tour. 4) New design file.
> 5) Press F for the frame tool, pick any Phone size on the right, click once to place it. 6) Rename the frame
> to "Before-After" plus your app name. Add a box at the bottom: "That is all today. We build tomorrow. If
> sign-in fails, keep going on paper, you will not be behind." One slide, large readable text.

### Slide 11: If You Finish Early

> Create one slide for a high school design class titled "Teardown Done?" Numbered list: 1) Tear down a
> screen that works WELL and name why it works. Good design is harder to see than bad design. 2) Find the same
> app on a different device and note what they moved, added, or removed. 3) Start a list of what you would
> DELETE from your screen. Most broken screens are broken because nothing was ever taken away. One slide.

### Slide 12: Before You Leave

> Create one "Exit" slide titled "Before You Leave." Body: "You should have: a screen picked and approved, a
> journey with the break circled, five annotations in design language, and a Figma file with a phone frame in
> it. Tomorrow: wireframe your fix on paper, then start building. Optional quiz retake tomorrow whenever you
> are ready." One slide, large text.

## 2. Video & Sound: Script, storyboard, shot list

**Today's documents:** `courses/video-and-sound/planning/week-4/handouts/thu-preproduction-terms.md`,
`project.md` (Part B)
**Go over:** the three preproduction documents and how they differ, then narrow to one idea and write the
script and shot list. Terminology first, then breakout.

### Slide 1: As You Come In

> Create one "As You Come In" slide for a high school video class. Big title: "As You Come In." Body: "Open
> your brainstorm list from yesterday. Circle the ONE idea you actually want to make. If you only have one
> idea, add two more before you circle. Warm-up question to think about: what is the very first shot?" Large,
> clean. One slide.

### Slide 2: Standards + Today

> Create one standards and agenda slide for a high school video class titled "Today: Preproduction." List

> Then a short agenda: 1) The three documents: script, storyboard, shot list, 2) How to narrow to one idea,
> 3) Write your script, 4) Write your shot list. Large readable text. One slide.

### Slide 3: The three documents

> Create one slide for a high school video class titled "Three Documents, Three Jobs." Show a three column
> table. Columns: Document, Answers, Looks like. Row 1: SCRIPT, what is SAID and what HAPPENS, text in
> scenes. Row 2: STORYBOARD, what each shot LOOKS like, drawings one box per shot. Row 3: SHOT LIST, every
> shot we have to GET and what it needs, a table one row per shot. Caption underneath in large text: "The
> script is the words. The storyboard is the pictures. The shot list is the plan." One slide.

### Slide 4: The script

> Create one slide for a high school video class titled "The Script: Two Column." Show a two column table
> with headers VIDEO and AUDIO. Row 1: "Wide shot, empty hallway, early morning" and "Ambient hallway sound,
> footsteps in the distance." Row 2: "Close up, hands opening a locker" and "NARRATOR: Most people never
> notice the first ten minutes." Row 3: "Over the shoulder, student reading a note" and "Music fades up."
> Caption: "Video left, audio right, lined up. If you cannot SEE it or HEAR it, it does not belong in a
> script." One slide.

### Slide 5: The storyboard

> Create one slide for a high school video class titled "The Storyboard." Body: "One box per shot, drawn. You
> do NOT have to be able to draw, stick figures are the professional standard for a rough board. Each box
> needs: the framing, an arrow for any movement, and a label with the shot number, shot type, and a few
> words. Storyboards exist so everyone pictures the SAME shot. Find that out with a pencil, not with a camera
> and a lost afternoon." One slide, large text.

### Slide 6: The shot list

> Create one slide for a high school video class titled "The Shot List." Show a short example table with
> columns: number, Description of what we SEE, Type, Location, Equipment. Row 1: 1, "Hands opening the
> locker", Close up, Main hallway, camera and tripod. Row 2: 2, "Student walking away down the hall", Wide,
> Main hallway, camera. Row 3: 3, "Over the shoulder as they read the note", OTS, Main hallway, camera and
> tripod. Caption: "This is the one you HOLD while shooting and check off." One slide.

### Slide 7: Why the order is different

> Create one slide for a high school video class titled "Why the Shot List Is Out of Order." Body: "The
> script is in STORY order, the order the audience sees it. The shot list is in SHOOTING order, grouped by
> location. You shoot everything in the hallway while you are standing in the hallway, no matter where those
> shots land in the finished video. That is the single biggest time saver in production." One slide, large
> text.

### Slide 8: Shot types

> Create one slide for a high school video class titled "Shot Types." Show a two column table. Wide (WS):
> the whole scene and where you are. Medium (MS): waist up, the workhorse. Close up (CU): a face or object
> filling the frame. Extreme close up (ECU): a small detail, emphasis or tension. Over the shoulder (OTS):
> from behind one person looking at another. Cutaway: something else in the scene, used to cover an edit. One
> slide, clean table.

### Slide 9: How to narrow

> Create one slide for a high school video class titled "Pick the One." Body: "Ask three questions about
> each idea: (1) Can I actually film this at school, this month? (2) Do I know what the FIRST shot looks
> like? (3) Is something visually interesting happening, or is it just people talking? The idea that survives
> all three is your idea." One slide, large text.

### Slide 10: Do Now

> Create one "Do Now" slide for a high school video class. Big title: "Do Now: Script, Then Shot List."
> Numbered body: "1) Write a two column script for your idea, video left, audio right. 2) Turn it into a shot
> list: number, what we SEE, shot type, location, equipment. 3) Aim for 6 to 12 shots for a 30 to 90 second
> video. 4) Group the shot list by LOCATION, not story order." Large, clean. One slide.

### Slide 11: Turn and Talk

> Create one "Turn and Talk" slide for a high school video class. Big title: "Turn and Talk: Read It Back."
> Body: "Hand your shot list to a partner. They read it back to you in order, describing what they picture.
> If what they picture is not what you meant, your description is not specific enough. Fix those lines. Then
> quiz each other: which document tells you what a shot LOOKS like? Which one do you carry while shooting?"
> One slide.

### Slide 12: If You Finish Early

> Create one slide for a high school video class titled "Script and Shot List Done?" Numbered list: 1)
> Thumbnail sketch your first three shots, rough boxes and arrows. 2) Write your production schedule, what
> gets filmed on what day. 3) Mark the one shot you think will be hardest and why. 4) Add a cutaway for each
> location, you will want them in the edit and you will not think of it later. One slide.

### Slide 13: Before You Leave

> Create one "Exit" slide titled "Before You Leave." Body: "You should have one idea picked, a two column
> script, and a numbered shot list grouped by location. Tomorrow: storyboarding, plus the optional quiz
> retake whenever you are ready." One slide, large text.

## 4. Middle School CS: Variables and the win condition

**Today's documents:** `courses/middle-school-cs/planning/week-4/handouts/bell-ringer-thu.md`,
`handouts/thu-live-build-script.md`, `handouts/tilemap-maze-guide.md` (Part 5), `project.md`
**Go over:** the quiz warm-up bell ringer, then **build the counter and the win live on the board.**
**Quiz is tomorrow: if statements only, NOT tile maps.**

### Slide 1: As You Come In

> Create one "As You Come In" slide for a middle school computer science class. Big title: "As You Come In."
> Body: "Open your maze project and run it. Drive at a wall to check your collision still works. If your walls
> leak, fix that first, we build on top of the maze today. Then get out paper for the bell ringer." Large,
> clean, minimal. One slide.

### Slide 2: Standards + Today

> Create one agenda slide for a middle school computer science class titled "Today: Count What You Collect."
> Text: Today's skills are variables and conditionals, from the Ohio CS standards for Algorithmic Thinking and
> Programming. Agenda: 1) Bell ringer, the same kinds of questions as the quiz, 2) Watch me build the counter
> and the win, live, 3) Build yours. Add a box in large text: "Quiz TOMORROW on IF STATEMENTS and everything
> before. NOT tile maps." Large readable text. One slide.

### Slide 3: Bell Ringer, question 1

> Create one bell-ringer slide for a middle school computer science class titled "Bell Ringer 1: Trace It."
> Show this code in a large monospace box: "set score to 0", "set lives to 3", "change score by 7", "if score
> > 7: change lives by -1", "else: change score by 3". Below it in large text: "What are score and lives at
> the END?" One slide, large readable text.

*(Answer to reveal: score = 10, lives = 3. The trap: score is 7, and 7 > 7 is FALSE, so the else runs. Ask
what changes if the condition were `score >= 7`.)*

### Slide 4: Bell Ringer, questions 2 and 3

> Create one bell-ringer slide for a middle school computer science class titled "Bell Ringer 2 and 3." Two
> boxes. Box 1: "If x = 6, is x > 5 AND x < 6 true or false? What about x > 5 OR x < 6?" Box 2: "if score <
> 20 background blue, else if score < 40 background yellow, else background red. What color when score is
> exactly 20? What about exactly 40?" One slide, large readable text.

*(Answers: AND is FALSE (6 < 6 fails), OR is TRUE. 20 is yellow, 40 is red. Say out loud: a chain runs top to
bottom and stops at the first true condition.)*

### Slide 5: Bell Ringer, questions 4 and 5

> Create one bell-ringer slide for a middle school computer science class titled "Bell Ringer 4 and 5." Two
> boxes. Box 1: "score % 4 == 0 is true for which numbers? How would you make a bonus fire every 3 points?"
> Box 2: "In an if/else statement, how many branches run on a single pass?" One slide, large readable text.

*(Answers: 0, 4, 8, 12. Every 3 points is `score % 3 == 0`. Exactly ONE branch, never both, never neither.)*

### Slide 6: The four traps

> Create one slide for a middle school computer science class titled "The Four Traps." Numbered list in large
> text: 1) ">" is not ">=". When the number is exactly equal, ">" is false. 2) AND needs BOTH sides true. OR
> needs only ONE. 3) A chain stops at the FIRST true condition, it does not keep checking. 4) "%" is about
> the remainder, so "% 5 == 0" means "every 5." Add a caption: "These are on the quiz." One slide.

### Slide 7: Watch me build it

> Create one slide for a middle school computer science class titled "Watch, Hands Off." Body: "I am going to
> build the counter and the win condition on the board, from nothing. Hands off your keyboards. You will get
> work time right after. Watch for the moment it BREAKS, and see if you can tell me why before I do." One
> slide, large text.

### Slide 8: The code, line by line

> Create one slide for a middle school computer science class titled "What Each Line Does." Show the code in
> a monospace box: "let keys = 0", "on overlap Player with Food:", indented "otherSprite.destroy()", "keys +=
> 1", "info.setScore(keys)", "if keys == 5: game over WIN". Then a list underneath: "let keys = 0" makes the
> counter and starts it at zero; "on overlap" runs every time the player touches an item; "destroy" removes
> the item you just touched; "keys += 1" adds one to your count, this is the variable doing its job; the "if"
> checks whether you have them all yet. One slide, large legible text.

### Slide 9: The most common bug

> Create one slide for a middle school computer science class titled "Why Your Win Never Happens." Body: "The
> number in your if statement has to MATCH how many items you actually placed. If your if says 5 and you put
> 7 items in the maze, the player can never trigger the win, because the counter blows past 5 without ever
> equaling it at the right moment. Count your items. Fix the number." One slide, large text.

### Slide 10: Do Now

> Create one "Do Now" slide for a middle school computer science class. Big title: "Do Now: Make It
> Winnable." Body: "1) Place your items around the maze. 2) Add the counter variable. 3) Add the on-overlap
> that destroys the item and adds one. 4) Add the if that ends the game as a win. 5) TEST IT: collect
> everything and confirm you actually win. Count your items and match the number in your if." Large, clean.
> One slide.

### Slide 11: Turn and Talk

> Create one "Turn and Talk" slide for a middle school computer science class. Big title: "Turn and Talk: Can
> They Win It?" Body: "Trade seats and play your partner's maze all the way through. Can you collect
> everything and actually trigger the win? If the win never fires, tell them, and check their number against
> how many items are really in the maze." One slide, large readable text.

### Slide 12: If You Finish Early

> Create one slide for a middle school computer science class titled "Winnable? Level It Up." Numbered list:
> 1) Make the maze harder, dead ends and longer paths. 2) Swap your tile art to a different theme. 3) Add an
> enemy that costs you a life with an if. 4) Add a second level that loads a new tile map. 5) Add a timer that
> ends the game if it runs out. One slide.

### Slide 13: Before You Leave

> Create one "Exit" slide titled "Before You Leave." Body: "Your maze should be winnable: items placed,
> counter counting, and an if that ends the game. Tomorrow: the QUIZ on if statements and everything before,
> NOT tile maps, then work time on the maze. Go back over the four traps tonight." One slide, large text.
