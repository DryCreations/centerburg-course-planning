## 1. Design Techniques: What feedback is

**Documents:** `week-6/handouts/what-feedback-is.md` (student-facing, post it),
`week-6/handouts/peer-review.md` (still live), `week-5/handouts/interaction-labs.md` (Labs 6 and 7)
**Before class:** pull 4 to 6 real replies off yesterday's Classroom Question, **names removed**, into a
slide or a doc. Two that meet the bar if any do, and the rest that do not.
**Run it as:** the sort on the board, about 15 minutes, then rewrite the replies, then the demos, then
build. The rewrite is what gets graded, not yesterday's version.

### Slide 1: Standards today

> Create one standards slide for a high school design class titled "Today's Standards." List these with
> their FULL text, not just the numbers, in large readable type: 2.15.1 Understand the UX/UI design
> process (vision, journey mapping, wireframing, prototyping) for the targeted platform. 7.1.2 Describe
> the components of interactive media. 7.2.10 Select and create visual design elements appropriate for the
> intended audience and use. Then a short line: "Today that means giving feedback someone can actually use,
> and connecting every screen." Design it to stay readable on the board all period. One slide.

### Slide 2: The test

> Create one slide for a high school design class. Big title: "The Test." In very large text in the
> middle: "If your reply could have been written WITHOUT opening the link, it is not feedback." Below it,
> smaller, a row of examples that all fail that test: "Looks good." "Nice colors." "Maybe add more." "I
> like the layout." Then one line at the bottom: "Apply this to your own reply before you post it." Clean
> and stark. One slide.

### Slide 3: Sorted

> Create one comparison slide for a high school design class titled "Does Not Count / Counts." Two
> columns. Left column, DOES NOT COUNT, with short examples and a reason each: "Looks good" names nothing;
> "I like the colors" is a preference, not an observation; "Maybe add more stuff" more what, where, why;
> "It's confusing" names a feeling, not a screen. Right column, COUNTS, with one full example written out:
> "When I tapped the three lines in the top left, I expected a menu, but it went back to the home screen.
> I thought it was a menu because that is where menus usually sit. An actual menu icon, or the word
> Filter, would have told me." Add a line under it: "Every good one names a THING on a SCREEN. Not the
> project, not the person, not the vibe." One slide.

### Slide 4: Why this is the grade

> Create one slide for a high school design class titled "Why The Reviewing Is The Skill." Short body:
> you cannot find the problems in your own prototype because you already know what everything is supposed
> to do; practicing on someone else's work is how you build the eye that catches it in yours; a person who
> gives real feedback is a person people bring work to, and a person who says "looks good" gets asked
> once. Then a box in large text: "Yesterday a lot of us spent a full period producing nothing usable, for
> ourselves or for the person we replied to. Today the rewrite is the grade." One slide.

### Slide 5: Do it again, then build

> Create one "Do Now" slide for a high school design class titled "Rewrite It." Numbered: "1) Open the two
> links you replied to. 2) Rewrite your reply, three or more sentences, covering all four: what looks
> clickable, what you tried to tap that did nothing, what wins your eye on the main screen and whether it
> should, and one confusing thing plus what would fix it. 3) Check it against the test before you post. 4)
> Go talk to the person, get the demo, edit your reply with what changed. 5) Make at least one real change
> to your own file." Then a box: "WEDNESDAY is the real deadline. Every screen reachable, both directions.
> Thursday is for fixing, not building. Lab 7: Transitions if you are connected." One slide.

### Board version

## 2. Video & Sound: Filming, then CapCut

**Documents:** `week-6/handouts/capcut-first-timeline.md` (student-facing, post it),
`week-6/handouts/filming-signup.md` (unchanged, still running)
**Run it as:** sign-ups first, three filming slots as yesterday. Everyone not in a slot offloads footage
and builds a timeline in CapCut. Groups with no footage yet do missing work first, then borrow a clip.
**CapCut is installed on the Macs.** No Resolve yet.

### Slide 1: Standards today

> Create one standards slide for a high school video production class titled "Today's Standards." List
> these with their FULL text, not just the numbers, in large readable type: 7.7 Apply video production
> techniques. 7.3.7 Edit video footage. 7.7.5 Select a linear or nonlinear editing system and edit the
> video. 7.7.8 Export video to the desired medium. 2.10 Operate equipment according to manufacturer
> specifications. Then a short line: "Today that means filming your slot, then cutting what you shot."
> Design it to stay readable on the board all period. One slide.

### Slide 2: Sign up, same as yesterday

> Create one slide for a high school video production class titled "Sign Up: Same As Yesterday." Show the
> three-slot board: three fifteen-minute slots across Camera A the T4i, Camera B the T5, Camera C the T6.
> Rules in short lines: groups who have not had a slot yet go first today; be ready when your slot starts,
> setup is your time; 1/60 shutter, 30fps, manual, white balance set; SILENT while another group records;
> camera back five minutes before the bell. Add a line: "Not in a slot? You are offloading and editing.
> See the other slide." One slide.

### Slide 3: Offload first, every time

> Create one slide for a high school video production class titled "Offload First." Numbered: "1) Camera
> or card reader connected. 2) Copy clips into YOUR GROUP'S FOLDER. Not the desktop. Not Downloads. 3)
> Name them so a stranger could find a shot: scene2_shot3_take1. 4) Eject the card properly before pulling
> it." Then a box in large text: "Footage that lives only on the card is footage the next group overwrites.
> Offload before you touch CapCut." One slide.

### Slide 4: Your first timeline

> Create one slide for a high school video production class titled "CapCut: Build A Timeline." First a
> short note: "No Resolve yet. The timeline, the playhead, trimming and ordering work the same in every
> editor ever made. Learn them here." Then the assignment, numbered, in large text: "1) Four or more of
> your clips on the timeline, in an order that makes sense, not the order you shot them. 2) Trim the dead
> time off both ends of every clip. 3) Close the gaps, no black. 4) Export at 1080p into your group
> folder. 5) Three sentences on Classroom: which cut works best and why, which cut feels wrong and what
> would fix it." Add a box: "EVERYONE in the group does this. Not one person driving while two watch."
> One slide.

### Slide 5: Rules, and the four moves

> Create one slide for a high school video production class. Title: "Four Moves, And Four Rules." Left
> side, "THE FOUR MOVES, in every editor there is": Scrub, drag the playhead to find a frame; Trim, drag a
> clip's edge to cut off what happens before and after; Split, playhead where you want the cut; Reorder,
> drag a clip left or right, because the order is the story. Right side, "TODAY'S RULES": your own footage
> only; no effects, no filters, no transitions, cuts only; no music, you need to hear your own audio;
> ignore every template and auto-everything CapCut offers you. Add a line under the rules: "Effects are
> how people avoid learning to cut. Cutting is the whole skill." One slide.

## 4. Middle School CS: Quiz-prep bell ringer, then build

**Documents:** `week-6/handouts/bell-ringers.md` (teacher reference, Wednesday quiz-prep set),
`week-6/handouts/wave-defender-lab.md` (Day 3: hits, misses, and ending)
**Run it as:** put up **two or three** of the five prep questions, not all five. Take the ones this class
is weakest on. Five minutes plus going over them, then lab work.

### Slide 1: Bell ringer

> Create one bell ringer slide for a middle school computer science class. Big title: "Bell Ringer: Quiz
> Prep." Leave a large open area where the teacher will paste two or three block-style code questions in
> monospace text. Add a subtitle: "Friday's quiz is loops, nested loops, while, for index, modulo,
> conditionals, variables, and debugging. These are the shapes you will see." Add a small line at the
> bottom: "Not graded. Getting it wrong right now is free." One slide.

### Slide 2: The two that get missed

> Create one slide for a middle school computer science class titled "The Two Everybody Misses." Two big
> boxes. Box one: "for index from 0 to 3 runs FOUR times. Count the zero. Zero, one, two, three." Box two:
> "A loop inside a loop MULTIPLIES. repeat 3 with repeat 6 inside makes 18, not 9. Inside means it runs
> all the way through, every single time." Large, simple, memorable. One slide.

### Slide 3: Today's build

> Create one slide for a middle school computer science class titled "Today: Hits, Misses, And Ending."
> Numbered: "1) When a shot overlaps an alien, destroy BOTH. The alien and the projectile. 2) Add a point.
> 3) Decide what losing looks like and make it happen. 4) Decide what winning looks like and make it
> happen." Then a box headed "If it breaks": one shot clears a whole column, you only destroyed the alien;
> the game crawls, nothing is being cleaned up; shots fall instead of rising, your vy is positive, negative
> is up. One slide.

### Board version
