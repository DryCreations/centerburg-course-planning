## 1. Design Techniques: Two photos

**Documents:** **`week-8/handouts/two-photos.md`** (today's sheet, post it with both RAW files attached),
`week-8/teacher-notes.md`
**Five questions, four steps, one page.** Step 1 opens both files, step 2 is the silent review with no
questions attached, step 3 is push all four new sliders both ways and answer four reflection questions,
step 4 is decide final values on both and export. **The Thursday and Friday work is a separate sheet.**
**Attach both RAW files to today's Classroom assignment.** A hazy cityscape and a backlit portrait with
grass and cloud behind. **Slide 1 goes up before the bell** and holds the download and open instructions,
so the room starts working without you.
**The first ten minutes are theirs, with no questions attached.** Everything from Monday and Tuesday, on
both photos, in order, making their own calls. **Do not walk them through it.** The point is finding out
what they can do unassisted.
**Then the discussion**, which is the middle of the period: look-fors, artistic choices, and reading the
histogram shape rather than centering it.
**Then Dehaze**, on both photos, which is the one real experiment of the day. **The same slider at the
same value does two different things**, and question 1 asks why. That is the question worth the most.
**Then Clarity on both**, which is cheap to add because both files are already open: the cityscape is
Clarity's best case and the portrait is its worst.
**Then Saturation against Vibrance** on the portrait, where skin and grass and sky all respond
differently.
**Questions are deliberately few, and none are tied to the review stage.** They hand in both finished
JPEGs plus one short paragraph per photo about their own choices. **Not a slider-by-slider account.**
**Say "push it all the way up, then all the way down, then back to zero" out loud** before step 3. The
answers only exist if they actually went to both ends.
**Warn them not to sync settings** between the two files. The filmstrip offers it and the photos need
different treatment.

### Slide 1: As you come in

> Create one instruction slide for a high school design class titled "Start Without Me." Four numbered
> steps in large, very readable text, designed to be followed with no explanation: "1. Download BOTH files
> attached to today's assignment, into your own folder. 2. Select both: click one, hold SHIFT, click the
> other. 3. Right-click, Open With, Photoshop. 4. Both photos open in Camera Raw, with thumbnails in a
> FILMSTRIP down the left side. Click a thumbnail to switch between them." Then a box in very large text:
> "You are editing BOTH photos today. They need different things. DO NOT sync settings between them."
> One slide.

### Slide 2: Ten minutes. Everything you already know.

> Create one task slide for a high school photography class titled "Ten Minutes. On Your Own." Body:
> everything from Monday and Tuesday, on both photos, in order. Nobody is walking you through it. Then a
> checklist in large text: "Clipping warnings ON, both triangles. TEMPERATURE, and Tint if it needs it.
> EXPOSURE. CONTRAST. WHITES, then BLACKS, to set your two ends. HIGHLIGHTS, then SHADOWS, to recover what
> is inside them." Then a box: "Look at the histogram on each one. Decide whether the shape is WHAT YOU
> WANT. Not whether it is centered." Then a line in very large text: "Make your own calls. Then we talk
> about them. There are no worksheet questions for this part." One slide.

### Slide 3: The histogram has no correct shape

> Create one concept slide for a high school photography class titled "There Is No Correct Histogram."
> Show three small histogram diagrams in a row with captions. First, data piled to the left, captioned "A
> low-key photo SHOULD look like this." Second, data piled to the right, captioned "A snow scene SHOULD
> look like this." Third, data spread across the middle reaching both ends, captioned "And so should a
> normal daylight shot." Then in very large text: "The question is whether the shape matches what the
> photo is ABOUT. Not whether it is centered." Then a box: "A GAP at either end means you are not using
> that part of the range. The photo looks dull, and that is usually worth fixing. A WALL means detail is
> gone, and that is a decision you are making." One slide.

### Slide 4: Look-fors

> Create one slide for a high school design class titled "What To Actually Look For." A short list of
> questions in large text, each on its own line: "Where does your eye go first? Did your edit help that,
> or fight it?" "Is there one thing that is properly black? An image with no true black reads foggy."
> "Is anything you CARE ABOUT clipped? A bulb or a sun glint is free. A face is not." "Toggle the edits
> off and on. Can you see the difference from across the room?" Then a box in very large text: "Go too
> far, then come back about twenty percent. Almost everyone under-edits the first pass and over-edits the
> second." One slide.

### Slide 5: Dehaze, on both

> Create one task slide for a high school photography class titled "Dehaze. Try It On Both." Explain in
> one line that Dehaze is built to cut through atmospheric haze, and that it lives in the Basic panel, or
> the FX panel in older versions. Then three numbered tasks: "1. Push it UP on the CITYSCAPE. What
> happened? Look at the distance, not the foreground. 2. Push it up the SAME AMOUNT on the PORTRAIT. It
> does not behave the same way. Describe the difference. 3. Take it BELOW ZERO on both. What does it add?
> Which photo does that actually suit?" Then a box in very large text: "The same slider, at the same
> number, doing two different things. QUESTION 1 asks why. That one is worth the most." One slide.

### Slide 6: Clarity, on both

> Create one task slide for a high school photography class titled "Clarity." Explain in one line that
> Clarity is midtone contrast: it works on edges and texture rather than on the whole image. Then three
> numbered tasks: "1. Push it UP HARD on the CITYSCAPE. What is it doing, and where is it most obvious?
> 2. Now Clarity to +100 on the PERSON. Describe exactly what it does to skin. Then try it well BELOW
> ZERO on the same photo. What is that good for? 3. Dehaze and Clarity both make a photo look punchier.
> How are they DIFFERENT?"
> Then a box in very large text: "Watch the edges against the sky. That bright outline along a roofline or
> around hair is called a HALO, and it is how you spot an over-processed photo from across the room." One
> slide.

### Slide 7: Saturation against Vibrance

> Create one task slide for a high school photography class titled "Saturation vs Vibrance." Use the
> portrait. Two numbered steps in large text: "1. SATURATION to +60. Look at three places: the sky, the
> grass, and the person's skin. What happened to each? 2. Undo. VIBRANCE to +60 instead. Same three
> places. What is different?" Then the explanation underneath, in two short lines: "SATURATION pushes
> every color equally. The strong ones go first, and they go to mush." and "VIBRANCE pushes the DULL
> colors and mostly leaves strong ones alone. It also protects skin." Then a box in large text: "Reach for
> Vibrance first. Add Saturation only if it still needs it. Most photos do not." One slide.

### Slide 8: Finish and hand in

> Create one task slide for a high school design class titled "Finish Both. Then Tell Me Why." Body: set
> everything where you actually want it, on both photos, and you may go back and change earlier
> decisions. Then the exports, in monospace: "LastName_City_01" and "LastName_Portrait_01". Then the
> written part in a box, in large text: "QUESTION 5: ONE SHORT PARAGRAPH PER PHOTO. Not a list of sliders. What were
> you going for, and which two or three decisions mattered most to getting there? If something fought
> you, say what." One slide.

## 2. Video & Sound: Split edits

**Documents:** `week-7/handouts/split-edits-lab.md`, `week-8/handouts/layering-and-audio.md`,
`week-7/project.md`
**Run it as:** ten minutes at the front, then work time, same as yesterday.
**Today is the one technique the whole project was built around.** J-cuts and L-cuts are the reason the
footage was chosen.
**The deliverable is a log**, not a feeling: timecode, J or L, and how much offset. **Three of each.**
**Circulate for one thing:** is the offset big enough to hear? Two frames is not a J-cut, it is a
mistake. Tenths of a second, not frames.

### Slide 1: Standards and agenda

> Create ONE slide for a high school video production class that holds both the standards and the agenda.
> Title: "Today." Top half, headed "What we are covering," lists these with their FULL text, not just the
> numbers: 7.3.7 Edit video footage. 7.7.5 Select a linear or nonlinear editing system and edit the video.
> 7.8.8 Perform audio mixing. Bottom half, headed "What we are doing," is a short two-column list pairing
> each activity with its competency: "Three J-cuts and three L-cuts" with 7.3.7; "Logged with timecodes
> and offsets" with 7.7.5; "Levels balanced once there is more than one audio track" with 7.8.8. One
> slide.

### Slide 2: J and L, in one picture

> Create one diagram slide for a high school video editing class titled "J-Cut and L-Cut." Show a simple
> two-track timeline diagram twice. First, labeled "J-CUT": the video track cuts at one point, and the
> audio track of the SECOND clip starts EARLIER than its picture, so you hear the next scene before you
> see it. Caption: "You hear it before you see it. Used to pull the viewer forward." Second, labeled
> "L-CUT": the video cuts, but the audio of the FIRST clip KEEPS RUNNING under the new picture. Caption:
> "You keep hearing it after you have moved on. Used to hold a reaction." Then a box in large text: "Both
> are just dragging one edge past the other, after you detach the audio." One slide.

### Slide 3: Today's deliverable

> Create one task slide for a high school video class titled "Three Of Each. Logged." Body: three J-cuts
> and three L-cuts in your edit, and a log of all six. Then what each log line needs, in large text:
> "TIMECODE. J or L. How much offset." Then a box in very large text: "Offsets are in TENTHS OF A SECOND,
> not frames. Two frames is not a J-cut, it is a mistake. If you cannot hear it, it is not there." One
> slide.

## 3. Aviation UAS: The hazard list

**Documents:** `week-8/handouts/weather-brief-project.md` (Section 3, questions 19 to 30),
`week-8/teacher-notes.md`
**Same shape as Tuesday, which worked:** vocab and the things they cannot infer up front, then **half the
period on the worksheet.**
**Today is the heaviest content day of the week**, so the front half is tighter. Do not let it eat the
work time: **Section 3 ends in their own go or no-go numbers, and those go outside tomorrow.**
**Two things they genuinely cannot work out alone**, and everything else they can: **the three
thunderstorm stages**, and **the 20 mile rule.** Teach those directly.
**Everything else is vocabulary plus their own site.** Crosswind, crab, windshear, the four turbulence
causes. Give the word, then ask where it happens here.
**Question 30 is the deliverable.** Real numbers, not adjectives. Collect it.

### Slide 1: Standards and agenda

> Create ONE slide for a high school aviation and drone class that holds both the competencies and the
> agenda. Title: "Today: What Can Stop You." Top half, headed "What we are covering," lists these with
> their FULL text, not just the numbers: 7.6.13 Describe wind and wind effects (i.e. crosswind, tailwind,
> windshear, mountain wave). 7.6.11 Describe the types, conditions and factors of turbulence. 7.6.12
> Describe the types and impact of thunderstorms, tornados, microbursts and hurricanes. Bottom half,
> headed "What we are doing," is a short two-column list pairing each activity with its competency: "Wind
> words, and where each one happens at OUR site" with 7.6.13; "The four causes of turbulence" with
> 7.6.11; "Thunderstorm stages, and how far is far enough" with 7.6.12; "Then HALF THE PERIOD on Section
> 3" with 7.6.13. Then one line at the bottom in bold: "Section 3 ends in YOUR go or no-go numbers. You
> take them outside tomorrow." One slide.

### Slide 2: Where we are

> Create one slide for a high school drone class titled "Three Sections In, One To Go." A short list
> showing the brief so far: "MONDAY, Section 1: the site and the air. Is today's air working for you or
> against you?" "TUESDAY, Section 2: the system moving through. What is coming, and does the window
> move?" "TODAY, Section 3: the hazard list. What could actually stop you, with numbers." Then a box in
> very large text: "Tomorrow you fly with your own numbers in your hand. Today you write them." One slide.

### Slide 3: Wind has three names

> Create one slide for a high school drone class titled "Same Wind. Three Names." Explain that the name
> depends on where you are going, not on the wind. Three short entries: "HEADWIND: blowing at you. Slows
> you down, and that is the safe direction to be surprised in." "TAILWIND: blowing from behind. You go
> faster than you think, and you have less control stopping." "CROSSWIND: blowing across your path. The
> aircraft has to TILT INTO IT just to fly straight, which is called crabbing, and that tilt costs you
> battery and lift." Then a prompt box headed "TURN AND TALK": "You fly out with a tailwind and it feels
> great. Why is the trip back the part that strands you?" One slide.

### Slide 4: Windshear

> Create one slide for a high school drone class titled "Windshear." One definition in very large text:
> "A SUDDEN change in wind speed or direction, over a SHORT distance." Then the comparison: "Steady 20mph
> wind: the aircraft trims for it and holds. You can plan around steady." and "20mph that appears in one
> second: nothing had time to correct. That is what drops an aircraft." Then a prompt box headed "LOOK OUT
> THE WINDOW AND TELL ME": "Where at our site would you fly from still air straight into moving air?"
> Then a smaller hint line: "Think about corners of buildings, gaps between them, the edge of the
> treeline." One slide.

### Slide 5: The four causes of turbulence

> Create one slide for a high school drone class titled "Four Causes. One Example Each." A four-row table
> with the cause and a plain description: "MECHANICAL: air broken up by something solid in the way.
> Buildings, trees, fences." "THERMAL: bubbles of warm air rising off a hot surface. Pavement on a sunny
> afternoon." "FRONTAL: where two air masses meet. Yesterday's fronts." "WINDSHEAR: the sudden change you
> just saw." Then a prompt box headed "TELL ME, FOR OUR SITE": "Which one do you get beside the gym? Which
> one over the parking lot at 2pm?" One slide.

### Slide 6: Thunderstorm stages

> Create one concept slide for a high school aviation class titled "Three Stages. One Of Them Kills You."
> Three blocks in order, each saying what the air is doing: "CUMULUS: air is RISING. The cloud is
> building. It looks like weather is coming, because it is." "MATURE: air is rising AND falling at the
> same time, hard, right next to each other. Rain, hail, lightning. THIS IS THE DANGEROUS ONE." "DISSIPATING:
> air is mostly SINKING. The storm is dying, and it is still raining on you." Then a box in large text:
> "Rising next to falling is the part that matters. It is not the rain that takes an aircraft, it is the
> air going two directions at once." One slide.

### Slide 7: How far is far enough

> Create one slide for a high school aviation class titled "Twenty Miles." Open with the question: "A
> thunderstorm is 15 miles away and the sky above you is clear. Launch?" Then the answer in very large
> text: "NO. Standard aviation guidance is to stay TWENTY MILES from a thunderstorm." Then why, in three
> short lines: "The gust front runs out ahead of the storm, well beyond the rain." "Lightning strikes
> outside the cloud." "It is moving, and it may be moving at you." Then a box: "Clear sky overhead tells
> you nothing about what is arriving in ten minutes." One slide.

### Slide 8: Microburst

> Create one slide for a high school drone class titled "The Worst Thing On The List." Definition in large
> text: "A MICROBURST is a column of air falling fast, that hits the ground and spreads out violently in
> every direction." Then why it is the worst for a small aircraft, in two lines: "You fly into the
> headwind side and get lifted. You correct DOWN. Seconds later you are on the other side, in a tailwind,
> already pointed down." and "It is small, it is brief, and it is usually invisible." Then a prompt box
> headed "TELL ME": "What might warn you that one is near?" Then a small hint: "Rain that looks like it is
> spreading sideways at the bottom. Dust kicked up in a ring. A storm overhead or just past." One slide.

### Slide 9: Now write your limits

> Create one work slide for a high school drone class titled "Rest Of The Period: Section 3." Body:
> questions 19 to 30, and question 30 is the one that matters. Then what question 30 asks for, as a short
> list in large text: "YOUR numbers. Wind speed. Gust. Visibility. Ceiling. Temperature. Anything else you
> will not fly past." Then a box in very large text: "These are the numbers you bring outside tomorrow.
> Write limits you are actually willing to hold to, because tomorrow you have to." Then a line: "A limit
> is a number. 'Not too windy' is not a limit." One slide.

## 4. Middle School CS: Dialogue, and lists that go together

**Documents:** `week-8/handouts/dialogue-lists.md` (post it today), `week-8/handouts/project-plan.md`
**Run it as:** first half this sheet, second half the project plan. **The plan is due tomorrow**, say that
at the start.
**The four blocks are in the Game drawer.** Two of them say things, two of them **hand something back**,
and that distinction is the first question on the sheet.
**`ask` gives back true or false**, so it drops straight into an `if`. That connection is worth making
explicitly: they have been writing conditions all month, and this is a condition that comes from a person.
**Parallel lists are the real lesson.** Two lists, same order, same index. Questions and answers.
**Then they break it on purpose:** add a question without its answer, then put an answer in the wrong
spot. The second one is worse, **because nothing errors.**
**This is not on Friday's quiz.** The quiz covers lists, loops, conditionals, variables and the planning
language. Dialogue is for their projects.

### Slide 1: Four blocks, two kinds

> Create one slide for a middle school computer science class titled "Blocks That Talk." A four-row table
> from the Game drawer, with what each does and what it gives back. "splash" / "a short message, press A
> and it goes away" / "nothing". "show long text" / "a longer message, with a layout: bottom, center, top,
> or full screen" / "nothing". "ask" / "a YES or NO question. A means yes, B means no" / "TRUE or FALSE".
> "ask for string" / "pops up a keyboard, the player types" / "THE TEXT THEY TYPED". Then a box in very
> large text: "The first two just SAY things. The last two HAND SOMETHING BACK, which means you can put
> the answer in a variable and use it." One slide.

### Slide 2: An answer you can test

> Create one slide for a middle school computer science class titled "A Condition That Comes From A
> Person." Show in large monospace text: "if  ask 'Do you want the sword?'  then" then indented "splash
> 'You take the sword.'" then "else" then indented "splash 'You leave it.'" Below it: "ask gives back TRUE
> or FALSE. That is exactly what an if block wants." Then a box in large text: "You have been writing
> conditions all month. This is the first one where the ANSWER COMES FROM THE PLAYER." One slide.

### Slide 3: Two lists that go together

> Create one slide for a middle school computer science class titled "Parallel Lists." Show two lists
> stacked so the columns line up visually, in monospace: a row labeled "questions" containing "What color
> is the sky?" and "How many legs on a spider?", and directly under it a row labeled "answers" containing
