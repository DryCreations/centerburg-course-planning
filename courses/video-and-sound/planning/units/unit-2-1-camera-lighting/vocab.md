# Vocabulary: Camera & Lighting

**Course:** Video and Sound (145110)
**Unit:** 2.1 — Camera & Lighting
**Weeks:** 10–12

---

## Terms

| Term | Definition | Related Standard | DOK |
|------|-----------|-----------------|-----|
| **Rule of thirds** | A composition guideline dividing the frame into a 3×3 grid — subjects are placed along the grid lines or at their intersections rather than centered | 7.7.1 | 1 |
| **Leading lines** | Visual elements in the frame (roads, fences, pathways) that guide the viewer's eye toward the subject | 7.7.1 | 2 |
| **Depth of field (DOF)** | The range of distance in front of the camera that appears acceptably sharp — shallow DOF = blurred background (bokeh); deep DOF = everything in focus | 7.7.2 | 2 |
| **Aperture (f-stop)** | The opening in a lens that controls how much light enters the camera — also the primary controller of depth of field; lower f-stop = wider aperture = shallower DOF | 7.7.2 | 2 |
| **Shutter speed** | The length of time the camera sensor is exposed to light during one frame — controls motion blur; typical video rule: shutter speed = 2× frame rate (180° shutter rule) | 7.7.2 | 2 |
| **ISO** | The camera sensor's sensitivity to light — higher ISO = brighter image but more grain (noise); lower ISO = cleaner image | 7.7.2 | 2 |
| **White balance** | A camera setting that adjusts color temperature so white objects appear white — matched to the lighting source (daylight, tungsten, fluorescent, custom) | 7.7.3 | 2 |
| **Frame rate** | The number of frames per second (fps) captured — 24fps gives a cinematic look; 30fps is standard broadcast; 60fps allows smooth slow-motion | 7.7.3 | 1 |
| **Camera angle** | The vertical position of the camera relative to the subject — eye level, high angle (looking down), low angle (looking up), Dutch (tilted) | 7.7.1 | 1 |
| **Shot type** | The distance and framing of the subject in the shot — extreme close-up, close-up, medium, medium-wide, wide, extreme wide | 7.7.1 | 1 |
| **3-point lighting** | A classic lighting setup using three sources: key light (primary), fill light (softens key-side shadows), and back/rim light (separates subject from background) | 7.3.3 | 1 |
| **Key light** | The main, strongest light source in a 3-point lighting setup — establishes the primary illumination and direction of shadows | 7.3.3 | 1 |
| **Fill light** | A secondary, softer light used to reduce the shadow created by the key light — controls contrast | 7.3.3 | 1 |
| **Back light / rim light** | A light placed behind and above the subject — creates a rim of light separating the subject from the background; adds depth | 7.3.3 | 1 |
| **Practical light** | A light source visible within the shot (a lamp, window, screen) — defines the look of the scene | 7.3.5 | 2 |
| **Motivated lighting** | Lighting design that appears to come from a logical, believable source within the scene context — mimics reality | 7.3.5 | 2 |
| **Natural vs. artificial light** | Natural light from the sun and sky — varies throughout the day; artificial light from studio instruments, LEDs, practical sources — controllable and consistent | 7.3.6 | 2 |
| **Color temperature** | A measurement (in Kelvin) of the warmth or coolness of a light source — 3200K is warm/tungsten; 5600K is daylight/cool | 7.3.6 | 2 |

## Bell Ringer Prompts

1. Look at this still photograph. Where is the subject placed relative to the rule of thirds? How does the composition affect how you feel looking at it?
2. What is the 180° shutter rule in video, and why do cinematographers follow it?
3. A student sets up an interview indoors. The subject has harsh shadows on one side of their face. What is the likely problem, and how would you fix it with 3-point lighting?
4. What is the difference between a high-angle and a low-angle shot? What feeling does each create?
5. You're shooting outside at noon in direct sunlight. What challenges do you face with exposure, white balance, and lighting? How can you address them?
