# The Edit Project

**Video & Sound Production (145110) | Starts Wednesday Sep 30, runs into next week**

**You are not shooting anything.** You are given a finished script and every shot that was filmed for
it. Your job is to decide **how it gets cut together**, plan it on paper, and then build it.

Professional editors do exactly this. The footage arrives, and every decision that remains is yours.

## The storyboard is the gate

> **Show me a complete, clear storyboard and you can start editing. Until then, I do not want to see
> CapCut open.**

That is the whole rule. It is not a punishment and it is not about speed: **whoever finishes the plan
first starts editing first.** If your storyboard is done Monday, you edit Monday.

"Complete" means every shot in order, framing visible, characters tellable apart, and the cut type
written between panels.

**We are not opening the editor until the plan exists.** That is not a delay, it is the point. An editor
who starts cutting without a plan spends four hours discovering what they should have decided in twenty
minutes.

## Phase 2: Read and log

**Before you touch anything.**

### Read the script, twice

1. First pass: what happens?
2. Second pass: **where does the emotional weight sit?** Which line is the one the scene is actually
   about?

### Watch every clip, once each

**All of them. Do not skip the ones that look boring.** The shot you skipped is the cutaway you need on
Friday.

### Log what you have, with timestamps

For every clip, write down:

- **File name**
- **Timestamp in and out**, like `0:42 to 1:15`
- **Which scene** it belongs to
- **Which angle** it is: wide, medium, close-up, over the shoulder
- **Usable?** yes or no, and why not

**If your project came as one long file**, the timestamps are the most important column. You are marking
where this file should be cut apart, so write down every point where a new shot or angle starts.

**This is a real job.** Assistant editors log footage all day, and it is the job that makes the editor's
job possible.

---

## Phase 3: The cut plan, on paper

**Mark up the script.** For every place you intend to cut:

1. **Draw a line** where the cut happens
2. **Write which shot** you are cutting to
3. **Write which type of cut** it is: straight, J, L, cutaway, match, cut on action
4. **If it is a J or L, write how much offset**, roughly. "Sound starts half a second early"

### The questions to answer as you go

| | |
|---|---|
| **Whose face do I want to be on right now?** | The person speaking, or the person reacting? |
| **Why am I cutting here?** | "Because the line ended" is a weak answer. "Because I want to see her hear it" is a real one |
| **What does the audience need to know?** | You cut to what they need to see next |
| **Where is the scene actually about something?** | Protect that moment. Let it breathe |

> **A cut is an instruction to the audience: look here now.** If you cannot say why you are giving that
> instruction, do not make the cut.

## Phase 4: The storyboard

**Sketch every shot in order**, in the order a viewer will see them.

### What matters

| | |
|---|---|
| **Framing** | How much of the person is in the frame, and where they sit in it. **This is what I am looking at** |
| **Who is in the shot** | Characters have to be tellable apart |
| **Cut points** | Marked between panels, with the type |

### What does not matter

- **Art quality. At all.** Stick figures are completely fine
- Faces, clothing detail, backgrounds beyond what identifies the location
- Perfect proportions

**Differentiate your characters somehow.** Different hair, a hat, a label with their name, a letter over
their head. **If I cannot tell who is who, the storyboard is not doing its job.**

### Each panel gets

```
  +---------------------------+
  |                           |
  |     [ your sketch ]       |
  |                           |
  +---------------------------+
   Shot: ___  Framing: ______
   Cut to next: straight / J / L / cutaway / match
```

**Between panels, write the cut type.** That is the part that makes this a cut plan rather than a set of
drawings.

### Framing vocabulary to use

| Term | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Wide** | Subject and surroundings |
| **Medium** | Roughly waist up |
| **Medium close-up** | Roughly chest up |
| **Close-up** | Face, or a detail filling the frame |
| **Over the shoulder** | Framed past one person onto another |
| **Two shot** | Two people in frame |

## Phase 5 and 6: Build it, then build it again

**Next week.** Your plan goes into the editor.

Then **cut the same footage a second time**, with different decisions. Different shot choices, different
timing, different cut types.

**Then decide which is better and say why.** That comparison is the most valuable thing in this whole
project, and it is only possible because everyone starts from identical footage.

## Why plan before editing

Because **an editor with a plan makes decisions, and an editor without one makes whatever the timeline
suggests.**

You have a finished script and complete coverage. That is a better starting position than most
professional editors get, and if you open the editor first you will waste it.
