## Framing worth saying out loud this week

They have spent two weeks learning **where the buttons are**. That is not video production, and they
know it.

> **"You have been learning the tool. This week is the craft."**

Every technique this week works identically in Premiere, Resolve, and Final Cut. **None of them are
CapCut features.** Saying that changes how the week is received: it stops being "more CapCut" and starts
being transferable.

## Tuesday: the cuts. This is the best day of the week.

`handouts/how-cuts-happen.md`. Straight cut, J-cut, L-cut, cutaway, match cut, jump cut, cut on action.

**Demo on the projector, then they apply four to their own edit.**

The two that will actually change their work:

- **J-cuts and L-cuts.** Unlink the audio, drag one edge past the other by half a second. Small
  adjustment, large effect, and it is the real answer to "why does professional dialogue feel smooth and
  mine does not." Say that out loud, because they have all noticed the gap and assumed it was the camera
- **Cutting on action.** The single easiest upgrade to any edit and almost nobody does it. Beginners cut
  when a shot "is done," which is exactly when the cut is most visible

**Make them log it**: timecode, which technique, and whether it helped. **"It made it worse" is a
legitimate answer** and it is worth saying so, because a student who tries a J-cut, hates it, and can
say why has learned more than one who applied it because they were told to.

## Wednesday: color, and the features they have not touched

**Order is exposure, contrast, white balance, saturation.** They will skip to saturation because it is
the most visible slider.

The rule: **if you can tell a shot has been graded, you went too far.**

The distinction that keeps your filter ban coherent: **color correction makes footage look like what
your eye saw; a filter is a look applied without a decision.** One is craft, the other is avoidance.

**The assignment is concrete:** find two shots that should match and do not, and make them match. "Explore
color" produces purple footage.

**Push keyframes** out of the feature list. They work identically in every editor, they are how any
setting gets animated anywhere, and a volume fade or a slow push on a still shot is a five minute win.

## Audio is deliberately small this week

Thirty seconds on levels, thirty on room tone, in the Wednesday handout. **That is it.**

If you want the one demo that is worth doing: play a clip with mismatched levels, then the fixed version,
on the room speakers. Students do not believe audio matters until they hear a volume jump, and thirty
seconds settles it.

**Room tone: most of them will have none.** That is fine and it is the point. Assign 30 seconds of it for
the next shoot.

## The screening, if you run it

**Same footage, different edits, watched side by side.** This is the single most efficient teaching
device available this week, because the variation makes every choice visible.

Run it strictly:

1. The cut plays **all the way through with no commentary.** Nobody talks, including the editor
2. **The editor says nothing first.** No "it's not done"
3. Room gives notes. **Then** the editor can ask questions

> **The editor talking first ruins the data.** Once they explain the shot, nobody can tell them whether
> it read on its own, and that is the only thing worth knowing.

**Make them use the vocabulary.** "It felt weird" gets sent back; "the cut at 0:14 is a jump cut" is the
standard. This is also live rehearsal for Friday's quiz, which is why the vocab day and the screening
day sit next to each other.

**Timing:** you will not get through everyone. Four or five cuts is a full period done properly, and
doing five well beats rushing twelve. Rotate who gets screened across future weeks.

**The peer note lands harder than yours.** "You have a detail shot that would cover that cut" is the
same information from me and from a classmate, and only one of those gets acted on.

## If you run next-project planning instead

They have now felt what bad coverage costs in the edit, which is the only moment pre-production is ever
persuasive.

> **Say it directly: every problem you hit this week was created two weeks ago, on the shoot.**

Have them start a packet: concept, a one-sentence goal, and a shot list that **includes B-roll on
purpose** this time. Tuesday's cutaway lesson is the argument, and it is fresh.

## Still individual

## The rules stay banned

No filters, no effects, no auto-anything, no templates. Titles, a fade, and volume adjustment are the
assignment.

The reason, stated plainly: **effects are how people avoid learning to cut.** A student who spends the
week on transitions has learned nothing and their piece will be worse.

## Friday quiz note: two 180 degree rules

The bank contains both the **shutter speed** rule (double the frame rate) and the **camera placement**
rule (the imaginary line). Unrelated concepts, same name, and students conflate them constantly.

**Both are on the bank on purpose.** If your cut of 20 includes both, say so before the quiz so it is a
test of understanding rather than of guessing which one you meant.
