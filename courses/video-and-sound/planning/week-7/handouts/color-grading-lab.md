# Color: Correction, Then Grading

**Video & Sound Production (145110) | Tuesday**

Two shots of the same scene, filmed four minutes apart, usually do not match. One is bluer. One is
darker. The audience cannot tell you why, but they feel that something is off.

**Today is one tool, done properly.** Not a tour.

---

## First: we watch everything

**Every piece gets 30 to 60 seconds on the screen.** Everyone, briefly. Nobody is singled out and nobody
is skipped.

While each one plays, you are looking for **one specific thing**:

> **Do the shots look like they belong to each other?**

Not whether you like it. Whether the shots match. Write down, for each piece you see, one of:

- **Matched.** Looks like one thing
- **Drifts.** Some shots are brighter, darker, warmer or cooler than others
- **One shot is the problem.** Everything matches except one

**That is the whole viewing task.** You will be surprised how often the answer is "drifts," including in
your own.

---

## Correction versus grading

These are two different jobs and mixing them up is why student color work goes wrong.

| | | Goal |
|---|---|---|
| **Color correction** | Making it look **right** | Shots match each other. White is white. Nothing is crushed to black or blown to white |
| **Color grading** | Making it look **like something** | A warm nostalgic feel, a cold clinical feel, a mood |

**Correct first. Always.** Grading an uncorrected shot means you are fighting the original problem with a
second problem on top of it.

> **Most of what makes professional footage look professional is correction, not grading.** The
> invisible work is the work.

---

## The order, and it is not optional

Doing these out of order means re-doing them.

### 1. Exposure / brightness

Get the overall brightness right first. **Everything else depends on this.**

- Too dark: detail in the shadows is gone and cannot be recovered by brightening, it just gets grey
- Too bright: highlights are blown to pure white and there is nothing there to bring back

### 2. Contrast

How far apart the darks and the lights are.

- Flat footage looks like fog until contrast is set
- Too much and faces turn into hard shapes with no detail

### 3. White balance / temperature

**Make white things look white.** This is what fixes an orange or blue cast.

- Find something in frame you know is white or grey: a wall, paper, a shirt
- Adjust temperature until it reads neutral
- **This single step fixes most "my footage looks weird" problems**

### 4. Saturation

How intense the colors are. **Last, and use less than you want to.**

Students reach for this first because it is the most visible slider. Doing it first means you are
saturating a cast instead of removing one.

---

## The assignment

### Part 1: Make two shots match (required)

1. Find **two shots in your piece that should match and do not.** Same scene, different look
2. Put them next to each other on the timeline so you can flip between them
3. Correct the worse one. **Exposure, contrast, white balance, saturation.** In that order
4. Flip back and forth until you cannot tell which was which
5. **Toggle the correction on and off.** If you cannot see a difference, go further. If it looks fake,
   back off

**This is the graded part.** Two shots, matched, in order.

### Part 2: Correct the whole piece (required)

Go through every clip. **You are not making it pretty. You are making it consistent.**

Watch it straight through afterward and ask the same question you asked during the screening: *do these
shots look like they belong to each other?*

### Part 3: One look (optional)

**Only after Parts 1 and 2.** Pick one direction and apply it to everything:

| Look | Roughly |
|---|---|
| **Warm** | Push temperature toward orange. Feels nostalgic, friendly, sunny |
| **Cool** | Push toward blue. Feels clinical, cold, tense, nighttime |
| **Desaturated** | Pull saturation down. Feels serious, documentary, bleak |
| **High contrast** | Deeper blacks, brighter whites. Feels dramatic, punchy |

**Consistently, or not at all.** One graded shot in an ungraded piece looks like a mistake.

---

## The rule that will save you

> **If you can tell a shot has been graded, you went too far.**

The exception is a look that is obviously deliberate: a flashback, a dream, a stylized sequence. Even
then, **the rest of the piece has to be neutral** for the stylized part to read as stylized.

---

## Why this is allowed when filters are not

Fair question, and the answer is worth understanding rather than memorizing.

| | |
|---|---|
| **A filter** | A look applied without a decision. You picked it off a menu because it looked cool |
| **Color correction** | Making the footage look like what your eye actually saw in the room |
| **Color grading** | A decision you can explain, applied on purpose, consistently |

**One is avoidance. The other two are craft.** If you can say what problem you were solving, it is
craft.

## If you finish everything

| | |
|---|---|
| **Match a shot to a reference** | Pull a frame from something you like and try to match your footage to it |
| **Grade one section differently** | A flashback or a different location, deliberately set apart |
| **Keyframe a color change** | Have the look shift over time, for example as someone walks from inside to outside. **Keyframes work identically in every editor** and this is the most transferable thing you can do today |
| **Fix an impossible shot** | Find your worst-exposed clip and see how far you can actually bring it back. Learning the limit is useful |

## Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Color correction** | Making it look right: shots match, white is white |
| **Color grading** | Making it look like something: a mood or style |
| **Exposure** | Overall brightness |
| **Contrast** | How far apart the darks and lights are |
| **White balance** | Setting so white looks white under that lighting |
| **Temperature** | How warm (orange) or cool (blue) the image reads |
| **Saturation** | How intense the colors are |
| **Cast** | An unwanted overall color tint across the image |
| **Crushed blacks** | Shadows pushed so dark that detail is gone permanently |
| **Blown highlights** | Brights pushed to pure white with nothing recoverable |
| **Keyframe** | A marker that makes a setting change over time |
| **Reference** | A frame or image you are trying to match |
