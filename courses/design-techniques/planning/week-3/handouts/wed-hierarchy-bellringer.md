# Do Now: Read the Hierarchy in This Poster

**Design Techniques · Week 3 · Wednesday Sep 2**

Show students an AI-generated mockup poster. Their bell ringer: **describe how the design uses hierarchy**, using
yesterday's tools (size, weight, contrast, color, alignment, spacing, type pairing). The sentence starters make
them write it the way they'll **write about their own poster on their portfolio.**

---

## Part 1: Generate the poster (teacher prompt for an AI image tool)

Paste this into an image generator (or an AI design tool). It's written to produce a poster with **obvious,
teachable hierarchy** so students have plenty to point at.

> A clean, modern event poster in portrait orientation with strong visual hierarchy. Big bold headline at the
> top that reads "FALL TALENT SHOW" in a heavy display font, clearly the largest element on the page. Below it,
> medium-sized text for the key details: "Friday, October 17 · 7:00 PM · Centerburg HS Auditorium." Small fine
> print near the bottom: "Tickets $5 at the door · Doors open 6:30 · All ages welcome." One bright accent color
> (deep orange) used only on the headline and one shape, against a mostly dark navy background for strong
> contrast. A single supporting image area (a spotlight or stage). Everything aligned to a clean grid with
> generous whitespace. Two fonts only: a bold display font for the headline and a simple sans-serif for the
> details. Flat, professional, minimal style, high contrast, easy to read from across a room.

**Want a different topic?** Swap the event: "SPRING BLOOD DRIVE," "ROBOTICS CLUB INTEREST MEETING," "WINTER
CANNED FOOD DRIVE." Keep the rest of the prompt the same so the hierarchy stays clean.

> Tip: generate 2-3 and pick the one with the clearest reading order. If a version has weak hierarchy (everything
> the same size), that's actually a useful "what's wrong here?" example too.

---

## Part 2: Bell ringer sentence starters (post these in Google Classroom)

**Do Now:** Look at the poster. Finish these sentences to explain how it uses hierarchy. Use the words from
yesterday. You do not have to use every one, pick the ones that fit.

1. The **first thing I see** (the focal point) is ______, because the designer made it stand out by ______.

2. This design uses **size** to create hierarchy: the headline that says "______" is the biggest, so I read it
   first, then ______, then ______.

3. This design uses **color** to create hierarchy: the ______ color stands out against the ______ background,
   which pulls my eye to ______.

4. This design uses **font weight or different fonts**: the bold ______ font on the headline makes "______" pop,
   while the ______ font is used for the smaller details.

5. This design uses **contrast**: ______ next to ______ makes the important part separate from the rest.

6. This design uses **alignment**: the elements line up along ______, which makes it feel organized and guides
   my eye.

7. This design uses **whitespace / spacing**: there is open space around ______, which helps it stand out and
   keeps the poster from feeling crowded.

8. The **reading order** is: first ______, then ______, then ______.

> **Why this matters:** this is exactly how you'll **write about your own poster on your portfolio.** Naming the
> tool and saying what it does ("I used size to make the headline the focal point") is what turns a design into
> evidence of a skill. Practice it here on someone else's design first.

---

## Teacher notes

- Post the poster image + the sentence starters as the Classroom **Do Now** as students come in.
- After a few minutes, have 2-3 students read a sentence aloud and point to the poster. Push them to name the
  **tool** and the **effect** ("size, so the headline is first"), not just "it looks nice."
- Then connect it to their build day: "Now go make your poster do the same thing, and you'll write these same
  sentences about it for your portfolio."
