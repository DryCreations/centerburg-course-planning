# Composition: Where Things Go In The Frame

**Course:** Design Techniques (145095) | **Unit 2.1 Photography**

You have spent six weeks on hierarchy, contrast, alignment, and where the eye goes. **A photograph is
the same problem with a different tool.** The difference is that you cannot move the elements. You move
yourself.

---

## The rules, and what each one is actually for

### 1. Rule of thirds

Split the frame into thirds, both directions. **Put the subject on a line, or where two lines cross.**

**Why it works:** dead center is static. Off center creates tension, and tension holds attention. This is
the same reason you did not center every element on your app screens.

**Break it when:** the subject is symmetrical and you want stillness. A face straight on, a building, a
road going away from you.

### 2. Leading lines

Lines in the scene that point at your subject. Hallways, railings, rows of lockers, shadows.

**Why it works:** the eye follows a line. This is hierarchy: you are choosing what gets looked at first.

### 3. Framing

Shoot **through** something. A doorway, a window, between two people, under a branch.

**Why it works:** it adds depth to a flat image and it isolates the subject. It is the photographic
version of whitespace.

### 4. Fill the frame

Get closer. Then get closer again.

**Why it works:** almost every beginner photo has too much nothing in it. **If the subject is small in
frame, the photo is about the room, not the subject.**

> **The most common fixable problem in student photography is standing too far away.**

### 5. Leading room

If someone is looking or moving in a direction, leave space **in front of** them.

**Why it works:** without it, they look crammed against the edge. Your eye wants somewhere for the motion
to go.

### 6. Headroom

A little space above the head. Not a lot.

**Why it matters:** too much and they sink to the bottom of the frame. Too little and it looks like a
mistake.

### 7. Background

**Look at the background before you press the shutter.** Every time.

Poles growing out of heads, a trash can, a bright window pulling the eye. You will not notice it while
shooting and you will see nothing else afterward.

### 8. Simplify

If you cannot say what the photo is about in one sentence, there is too much in it.

**Fix it by moving**, not by zooming: change your angle, get lower, get closer, wait for someone to walk
out of frame.

## The assignment: five frames

**Five photographs, each demonstrating one thing.** Same day, your choice of subject. Phone is fine.

| # | Get |
|---|---|
| 1 | **Rule of thirds.** Subject clearly off center, on a third |
| 2 | **Leading lines.** Something in the scene points at your subject |
| 3 | **Framing.** Shot through a doorway, window, or gap |
| 4 | **Fill the frame.** A detail, close enough that you cannot tell where you are |
| 5 | **Your choice**, and say which rule it uses |

**For each one, one sentence:** which rule, and what you did to get it. "I got low so the railing ran
toward her" is an answer. "It looks good" is not.

### Rules for the assignment

- **You have to move.** Five photos from one standing position is not the assignment
- Shoot far more than five. Turn in the five that work
- **No filters.** This is about the frame, not the processing
- Real subjects. People doing things beats objects on a desk

## Wednesday: the same subject, different light

**One subject. Four photographs. Only the light changes.**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Front lit** | Light behind you, on the subject. Flat and even, and usually boring |
| **Side lit** | Light from one side. **Shows texture and shape.** Usually the best one |
| **Back lit** | Light behind the subject. Silhouette, or a rim of light. Hard, and worth it |
| **Open shade** | Out of direct sun entirely. Soft, even, flattering |

Then answer: **which is best, and why?** There is no correct answer and there is a correct kind of
reasoning.

> **Photography is not about the subject. It is about the light on the subject.** Four photos of the
> same thing will make that argument better than any amount of explanation.

---

## Thursday: cull and critique

**Pick your three best.** Then defend them.

Culling is a real skill. Professionals shoot hundreds of frames for one usable photo, and knowing which
one is the job.

### Critique rules, same as our design critiques

- **Name what is in the frame**, not whether you liked it
- "The pole behind his head pulls my eye" beats "something feels off"
- "The subject is on the left third and the hallway lines run toward him" beats "good composition"
- Everyone gets feedback, everyone gives it

## Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Composition** | How elements are arranged in the frame |
| **Rule of thirds** | Placing the subject on a third line rather than center |
| **Leading lines** | Lines that direct the eye to the subject |
| **Framing** | Using something in the scene to surround the subject |
| **Headroom** | Space above the subject's head |
| **Leading room** | Space in front of a subject's gaze or motion |
| **Negative space** | Empty area around the subject. The photographic version of whitespace |
| **Foreground / midground / background** | The layers of depth in an image |
| **Front / side / back lighting** | Where the light is relative to the subject |
| **Open shade** | Even light out of direct sun |
| **Culling** | Selecting the keepers from everything you shot |

## Why this follows UX so naturally

Every rule on this page is a rule you already know under a different name.

| In your app | In a photograph |
|---|---|
| One primary action per screen | One subject per frame |
| Hierarchy: what gets looked at first | Leading lines, contrast, focus |
| Whitespace | Negative space |
| Alignment | Rule of thirds, horizon lines |
| Cutting what does not serve the task | Filling the frame, simplifying |

**You are not starting over. You are applying the same eye to a different surface.**
