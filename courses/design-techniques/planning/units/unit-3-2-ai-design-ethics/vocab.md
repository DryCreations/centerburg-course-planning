# Vocabulary: AI in Design & Generative AI Ethics

---

## Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Artificial intelligence (AI)** | Computer systems trained on data to perform tasks that typically require human intelligence — including image generation, text generation, and pattern recognition |
| **Generative AI** | An AI system that creates new content — images, text, audio, or video — based on patterns learned from large training datasets; output is new, not copied directly |
| **AI image generation** | The process of using generative AI to produce visual images from text prompts or image inputs — in Adobe Express, accessible via Generate AI → Generate image; external tools include Midjourney and DALL·E |
| **Text prompt** | The written instruction given to a generative AI system — quality and specificity of the prompt significantly affects the quality of the output |
| **Prompt engineering** | The practice of crafting precise, detailed prompts to guide AI toward a desired output — a learnable and increasingly professional skill |
| **Adobe Firefly** | Adobe's family of generative AI models — the underlying technology that powers AI image features in Adobe Express (text-to-image, generative fill, expand, etc.); trained on licensed content, making outputs commercially safe |
| **Training data** | The large dataset used to train an AI model — for image generation AI, this is typically a large collection of existing images |
| **Commercial licensing** | The legal permission to use AI-generated content in paid or public work — depends on whether the AI model's training data was legally licensed |
| **AI ethics** | The field examining the responsible, fair, and transparent use of AI systems — considers bias, attribution, consent, environmental impact, and job displacement |
| **Bias in AI** | Systematic errors in AI output caused by imbalanced or unrepresentative training data — can result in outputs that reinforce stereotypes or exclude groups |
| **Attribution** | Crediting the source of content or ideas — AI-generated content raises new attribution questions: who owns the work, and should it be disclosed? |
| **Disclosure** | The practice of transparently indicating when AI was used to create or assist in creating a piece of work — increasingly expected in academic and professional contexts |
| **AI-assisted design** | Using AI tools to augment (not replace) the designer's work — generating options, removing backgrounds, suggesting layouts — while keeping human creative control |
| **Human creative control** | The principle that an AI-generated output is the starting point, not the finished product — the human designer makes final decisions about selection, refinement, and use |
| **Environmental impact** | The energy consumption and carbon footprint of training and running large AI models — an ethical consideration in evaluating AI tools and usage patterns |

## Review Questions

1. What is the difference between AI and generative AI? Give an example of each.
2. You're using Adobe Express's AI image generation for a client project. What makes it appropriate for commercial use? What makes other AI image tools risky?
3. A prompt "make a cool poster" gives you generic results. How would you improve the prompt using prompt engineering principles?
4. What is AI bias, and can you give an example of how biased training data might show up in a design context?
5. If you used generative AI to create part of a design for a competition or client project, what ethical responsibilities do you have regarding attribution and disclosure?
