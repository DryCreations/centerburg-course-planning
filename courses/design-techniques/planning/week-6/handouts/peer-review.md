# Peer Review: On Google Classroom

**Course:** Design Techniques (145095) | **Week 6, Tuesday Sep 22**

Your prototype goes up as a link on Google Classroom. Two other people open it **cold**, with no
explanation from you, and tell you what they see. Then you demo it to them and they tell you what
changed. Then everybody makes at least one change.

---

## Step 1: Post your link (first 10 minutes)

Your file has to be openable by someone who is not you.

- [ ] Present mode opens on your **home screen**, not on a scratch frame
- [ ] Frames are named something real (`Home`, `Search`, `Confirm`), not `Frame 12`
- [ ] Share set to **Anyone with the link, can view**
- [ ] Copy the **prototype link**, the one from the Present view, not the edit link

Then post it as an answer on the Classroom **Question**. Your post is:

> **Your name. Your link. Nothing else.**

**Do not describe your app.** Not what it does, not what to tap, not "it's not done yet." The whole point
is that someone meets it the way a stranger would. If you explain it first, you learn nothing.

---

## Step 2: Reply to at least two people, cold

Pick two classmates' links. Open them and **use them without asking a single question.** Tap around.
Try to figure out what the app is for and how to do the main thing.

Then reply to their post. **At least three sentences**, and cover all four of these:

| | What to say |
|---|---|
| **1. What looks clickable** | What did you think you could tap? Name the actual things |
| **2. What should be clickable but isn't** | What did you try to tap that did nothing, or expected to exist and could not find? |
| **3. Hierarchy** | What wins your eye first on the main screen? Should it? If not, what should, and what would make it win? |
| **4. Clarity** | What one thing was confusing, and what would make it clearer? |

**Write about what is on the screen.** Not "cool colors." Not "looks good." Say what you saw, where you
looked, and what you expected.

Use this shape whenever you can. It takes the opinion out of it:

> **When I** [did a thing], **I expected** [something], **but** [what happened].

- Weak: "the menu is confusing."
- Strong: "When I tapped the three lines in the corner, I expected a menu, but it went back to the home
  screen. I thought the three lines were the main navigation because they are in the top left where a menu
  usually is."

- Weak: "I like the green button."
- Strong: "The green Order button won my eye first on the home screen, and I think that is right, because
  ordering is the main thing the app does. Nothing else on that screen competes with it."

**Two replies is the minimum.** More is better, and more is the fastest way to get better at seeing this
in your own work.

---

## Step 3: Then talk to them

Once you have written your cold feedback, go find the person. Now they demo it.

**Builder:** show them your one task, start to finish. Say what the screen is supposed to do and what you
were going for.

**Reviewer:** listen, then answer the real question out loud: **did anything change?**

- Did something you called confusing turn out to make sense once explained? **That is still a problem.**
  A real user does not get a demo. Say so.
- Did something you thought worked turn out to be doing something else entirely?
- Did they intend something you completely missed? Say what would have made you see it.

---

## Step 4: Finalize your feedback

Go back to your reply on Classroom and **edit it, or add a second reply**, with what the conversation
changed. One or two sentences is enough:

> "After the demo: the three lines are supposed to be a filter, not a menu. I did not get that on my own.
> An actual filter icon or the word Filter would have told me."

The final version of your reply is what gets graded.

---

## Step 5: Change something. Everybody.

You got feedback from at least two people. **Make at least one real change based on it.**

**"Mine is perfect" is not an option today.** If two people got stuck in the same place, that is not an
opinion any more, that is a problem with the screen. And if nobody got stuck, there is still a screen
where the hierarchy could be stronger, a label that could be clearer, or a thing that looks tappable and
is not.

Keep a running list. It goes in Friday's write-up and it is **10% of the project grade**:

| Feedback I got | From | Changed it / Not changing it because |
|---|---|---|
| | | |
| | | |

"Not changing it" is allowed. It needs a real reason, about the person using the app, and it counts as a
decision either way.

---

## Step 6: Check yourself against the rubric

Before you leave, go down this list on your own file. Anything unchecked is what you work on next.

**Requirements**

- [ ] **3 to 4 phone frames**, all named something real
- [ ] A clear **home or starting screen**, and Present opens on it
- [ ] **One primary action per screen**, obviously the most prominent thing on it
- [ ] **At least 4 different interaction types** (navigate, back, overlay, state change, scroll, selection,
      input, transition)
- [ ] **Every screen reachable**, and a way **back** from every screen
- [ ] **Consistent navigation**: things that repeat sit in the same place on every screen
- [ ] It **looks like a complete app**: tab bar, icons, a settings gear, even for things you are not building
- [ ] A **list of what you are showing but not implementing**
- [ ] **Paper wireframes** for every screen, made before you built it
- [ ] **Two partner tests**, with what you changed after each
- [ ] **Write-up** naming four decisions and what each one solves

**How it is weighted**

| Criteria | Weight |
|----------|--------|
| The prototype works: every screen reachable, interactions functioning | 30% |
| Hierarchy and consistency | 20% |
| Write-up: four decisions named with reasons | 15% |
| Scope and focus: one task, served by every screen | 15% |
| Paper wireframes done before building | 10% |
| **Partner tests, and changes made from them** | **10%** |

> **Where people lose points:** everything the same size, so nothing is the primary action. A screen with
> a button and nothing else, which nobody would believe is a real app. A back button that moves around.
> And a write-up that says what was built instead of why.
