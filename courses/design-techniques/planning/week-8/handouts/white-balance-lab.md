## Part 1: Find the white balance control

At the top of the Basic panel, above everything else.

**With a RAW file** you get two sliders and a number in Kelvin:

- **Temperature.** Blue at one end, yellow at the other
- **Tint.** Green at one end, magenta at the other

**1.** What Kelvin number does your photo open at?

**2.** Drag Temperature all the way down, then all the way up. **What color does the photo go at each
end?**

**3.** Do the same with Tint. What two colors are at the ends?

## Part 2: Fix one

Find a photo with something in it you **know** is white or gray. A wall, paper, a shirt, a ceiling tile.

**4.** Is that thing actually white in the photo, or does it have a color to it? Which color?

**5.** Adjust Temperature until it reads neutral. What Kelvin did you land on?

**6.** Now look at the rest of the photo. **Did fixing the white thing make everything else look right,
or did something else go wrong?**

### The eyedropper

There is a **White Balance tool**, an eyedropper near the top of the toolbar. **Click it, then click
something that should be gray.**

**7.** Try it. Did it get closer or further than your manual adjustment?

**8.** Click the eyedropper on something that is **not** gray, like a red shirt. What happens, and why?

## Part 3: In camera versus later

**9.** Open the dropdown above Temperature. On a RAW file you get presets: As Shot, Auto, Daylight,
Cloudy, Shade, Tungsten, Fluorescent, Flash. **Try Tungsten and then Shade on the same photo.** Describe
the difference.

**10.** Those are the same settings the camera has in its menu. **So what is the advantage of setting
white balance in the camera instead of here?**

**11.** And what is the advantage of leaving it and fixing it here?

## Part 4: RAW versus JPEG

**This is the part that matters.**

**12.** Open a **JPEG** in the same editor. Look at the Temperature slider. **What is different about
it?** Is it in Kelvin?

**13.** Push the JPEG's white balance as far as it goes in one direction. Then do the same to a RAW file.
**Which one survives it better?** Describe what happens to the worse one.

**14.** In one or two sentences: **why does a RAW file have more room to fix white balance than a JPEG?**

## Part 5: Shoot one on purpose

**Everyone takes at least one new photo today**, and you need a camera for this.

Photograph something white or gray **under the worst light in the room**: under the fluorescents, next to
a window, in a shadow.

**15.** Shoot it in **RAW**. What does the photo look like before you touch anything?

**16.** Fix it in the editor. What Kelvin did it need?

**17.** Was the light warmer or cooler than daylight? How do you know?

## Words for today

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **White balance** | Setting the image so things that are white look white |
| **Temperature** | The blue to yellow axis, measured in Kelvin |
| **Tint** | The green to magenta axis |
| **Kelvin** | The unit for color temperature. Lower is warmer looking in this slider |
| **Color cast** | An unwanted overall tint across the whole image |
| **Neutral** | A gray, white or black area with no color in it |
| **As Shot** | The white balance the camera chose when you pressed the shutter |
| **Non-destructive** | Your changes are stored as instructions. The original file is never altered |
