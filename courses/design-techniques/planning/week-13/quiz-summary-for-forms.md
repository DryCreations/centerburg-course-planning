# Design Techniques Quiz, Mon Nov 9: Summary for Google Forms

**Teacher-only.** Paste everything inside the box into the quiz generator with your own notes. Cut any
section you did not actually teach.

```
Create a multiple-choice quiz in Google Forms for a high school graphic design class (Design Techniques). 15 questions, 4 options each, one correct answer, marked in the answer key, 1 point each.

Rules for the questions:
- Test the CONTENT students need to do their projects, not the BPA competitions themselves. No questions about event names, rules, or deadlines.
- Write most questions as short situations ("You find a photo on Google Images...") rather than definitions.
- Every question has one objectively correct answer. No "best" answers that are a matter of opinion.
- Write all four options in the same form and about the same length. The correct answer should NOT usually be the longest option.
- No "all of the above" or "none of the above."
- Plain, short wording for high school students.

Mix: about 8 questions on copyright and the right to use, about 3 on typography, about 4 reviewing first quarter.

CONTENT: COPYRIGHT AND RIGHT TO USE (about 8 questions)
- Copyright is automatic: the creator owns their work the moment it is made. No registration or copyright symbol is needed.
- Copyright covers photos, art, logos, illustrations, music, video, and fonts.
- Giving credit is NOT permission. Crediting a photographer does not give you the right to use their photo.
- Changing someone else's work "enough" does not make it yours. Tracing or editing another logo is still not your design.
- What students CAN use: their own work; public domain work (no one owns it anymore); Creative Commons work, following its license (most require credit); free-license photo sites like Unsplash or Pexels, with the link saved; anything they have written permission to use.
- What students cannot use: Google Images or Pinterest images, other companies' logos, traced artwork, anything with an unknown license.
- Fair use: limited use without permission for commentary, criticism, teaching, or parody. It is decided case by case, so it is risky to count on for a competition.
- Model release: written permission from a person to use a photo of them in a promotion. A parent signs for anyone under 18. Owning the photo is not the same as having permission to use the person's likeness.
- Templates: typing your name into a ready-made logo template is not your own design.
- Fonts have licenses too. Adobe Fonts and Google Fonts allow use in projects and logos. A font with an unknown license should not be used.
- Students have the full Adobe suite: Illustrator for logos and graphics that must stay sharp at any size (vector), Photoshop for photo editing and mockups, InDesign for layouts with multiple pages.

CONTENT: TYPOGRAPHY (about 3 questions)
- Serif (small feet, traditional, print), sans serif (no feet, clean, screens), script (handwritten look, use sparingly), display (decorative, headlines only).
- Point: type size, 72 points is about one inch. Pica: 12 points. Em: a space as wide as the type size.
- Kerning: space between two letters. Tracking: space across a whole word or line. Leading: space between lines.
- Pairing: two families, one job each; a serif with a sans serif contrasts well; never two scripts.
- Type style guide: lists the fonts, sizes, colors, and spacing a project uses so every piece matches.

CONTENT: FIRST QUARTER REVIEW (about 4 questions)
- Vector (logos, scales to any size without blur) vs raster (photos, made of pixels, blurs when enlarged).
- RGB is for screens; CMYK is for print. An RGB design can print duller than it looked on screen.
- Visual hierarchy: size, weight, and color decide what people read first.
- Camera settings: fast shutter speed freezes motion; aperture controls depth of field; higher ISO brightens but adds noise; white balance fixes color casts.
- Camera Raw editing: Temperature fixes an orange or blue cast; Contrast fixes a flat photo; the histogram shows clipped shadows or highlights; Vibrance boosts dull colors while protecting skin tones, Saturation boosts everything; HSL changes one color's hue, saturation, or luminance.
- Color psychology: blues and grays feel cold or calm; reds and oranges feel warm or energetic.
```
