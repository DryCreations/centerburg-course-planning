## What went wrong Monday, and the fix

**They sat there while I talked, and the concepts did not land.** Those are the same problem. A long
stretch of talking gives nobody a reason to think, and a student who has not had to produce an answer
does not know they do not have one. Neither do you.

**Tuesday's fix is structural, not motivational:**

1. **One idea per slide, and nothing else on it.** If a slide has two ideas, it gets split.
2. **No stretch of teacher talk longer than six minutes.** Every beat ends in a question they answer,
   not a question you answer.
3. **Predict before reveal.** On all four fronts they commit to a guess from what they already know
   about dense air, then find out. A wrong prediction they wrote down teaches more than a correct slide.
4. **You put the real data on the projector.** Monday, hunting for a METAR ate time that was supposed to
   be spent thinking. Today the TAF and the map are already up.

### The turn-and-talk protocol, every time

Say it once at the start and then just run it. **Write it on the board.**

| | |
|---|---|
| **30 seconds** | Alone. **Write something down.** No talking |
| **60 seconds** | With your partner. Both of you say your answer out loud |
| **Share** | **I call on two people by name.** Not hands |

**The rule that makes it work:** when called on you may be asked for **your partner's** answer, not your
own. That is why both of you say it out loud. Say that rule at the start and then actually do it once in
the first beat, or the rest of the period will not believe you.

**If a pair finishes early**, they get the follow-up question printed on the slide. There is one on every
beat for exactly this reason.

## The two concepts that did not land, and why

### Why air moves

**The failure:** "warm air rises" is something they have heard since elementary school, so it sounds
finished. They nod and nothing happens.

**What is actually new:** that something has to **replace** the air that left, and that the replacing is
the wind. Push on the hole, not the rising.

**The question that does it:** *Asphalt is 130 degrees, the grass beside it is 85. The air over the
asphalt goes up. What takes its place, and where did it come from?*

**Listen for:** air from over the grass, moving sideways. **That sideways move is wind.** If a pair says
"nothing" or "more hot air," they have the gap, and now you can see it.

### Density altitude

**The failure:** it is a number with an unfamiliar name and the words "altitude" and "density" both
point the wrong way. They tried to make it mean how high they are.

**What it actually is:** **how thin the air is behaving, no matter where you are standing.** Lead with
that sentence and do not say the words "pressure altitude" at all this week.

**The question that does it:** *Same drone, same battery, same pilot. One flight on a 40-degree morning,
one on a 95-degree humid afternoon. Which one gives you fewer minutes in the air, and why?*

**Listen for:** thinner air, props have less to push against, so they work harder for the same lift.
**The connection to make out loud:** this is last week's payload lesson with the weight hidden. They
understood the payload version, so hang it on that.

**If it still is not landing:** ask what happens to a swimmer in water versus in air. Same stroke, less to
push against, less result.

## The four fronts: predict, then reveal

**Give them the one thing they need, then make them guess.** They already know dense air sinks and less
dense air rises. Every front follows from that.

| Front | **Ask first** | What they should land on |
|-------|---------------|--------------------------|
| **Cold** | Cold dense air arrives at a wall of warm air. **Which one goes up, and how fast?** | Warm air is shoved up steeply. Tall clouds, short violent weather, passes quickly, clears to cool and gusty |
| **Warm** | Warm light air arrives behind cold air sitting on the ground. **Can it push that out of the way?** | No. It rides up over the top on a long shallow slope. Hours of low cloud, drizzle, poor visibility, slow |
| **Stationary** | Neither one is winning. **What does your week look like?** | Nothing moves for days. Whatever you have, you keep |
| **Occluded** | A cold front catches up to a warm front. **Where does the warm air go?** | Lifted completely off the ground. Messy, mixed, the worst of both |

**The one-line summary they should write for Q15:** **cold fronts bring short and violent, warm fronts
bring long and dreary.**

**Tie it to flying every time**, in one sentence per front. A cold front means your flight window is
before it or two hours after it. A warm front means today is scrubbed and so is probably tomorrow
morning.

## Lows and highs

Keep it to two facts and one consequence:

- **Air circulates counterclockwise into a low**, and rises in the middle. Rising air makes clouds.
  **A low means weather.**
- **Air circulates clockwise out of a high**, and sinks in the middle. Sinking air kills clouds.
  **A high means a flyable day.**
- **The consequence:** a low on the map upwind of you is a deadline. You fly before it arrives.

**Do not teach the Coriolis explanation.** It is not on the standard, and it will eat ten minutes.

## What to collect

**Q18, the Section 2 verdict**, before they leave. Two sentences: what is coming, and does the flight
window need to move.

**You are grading it for one thing:** does the verdict name something specific from the TAF or the map?
"It might rain" is not a verdict. "The TAF has the ceiling dropping to 1,200 after 1800, so Thursday
afternoon is the window" is.
