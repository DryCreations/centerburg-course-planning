# Shutter Speed: Freeze It or Blur It

**Yearbook, Week 5.** Last time you learned aperture, which controls **how much is in focus.** This week:
shutter speed, which controls **how motion looks.**

This is the setting that decides whether the volleyball is frozen in midair or a smear.

---

## What it is

Shutter speed is **how long the sensor is exposed to light.** It is written as a fraction of a second.

| On the camera | Means | Motion looks |
|---------------|-------|--------------|
| `1/1000` | One thousandth of a second | Frozen solid |
| `1/500` | | Frozen |
| `1/250` | | Mostly frozen |
| `1/125` | | Walking is sharp, running is not |
| `1/60` | | Handheld limit for most people |
| `1/30` | | Visible blur, camera shake |
| `1/8` | | Heavy blur |
| `1"` | A full second | Streaks |

**Bigger bottom number means faster shutter means more frozen.** `1/1000` is much faster than `1/60`.

---

## The two things it controls at once

### 1. Motion

A fast shutter freezes. A slow shutter blurs. **Both are useful.** A frozen spike is a great sports photo. A
blurred set of hands at a potter's wheel says "this is moving" in a way a frozen one never will.

### 2. Brightness

The shutter is also how long light gets in. **Faster shutter means a darker photo.**

This is the trade nobody warns you about. You speed up the shutter to freeze the action, and suddenly
everything is too dark. Then you have to open the aperture or raise the ISO to get the light back.

> **This is the exposure triangle.** Aperture, shutter speed, ISO. Change one and you have to change another
> to keep the brightness the same.

---

## Numbers that actually work

| Situation | Start at |
|-----------|----------|
| Indoor sports, gym | `1/500` or faster |
| Outdoor sports, daylight | `1/1000` |
| Someone walking | `1/250` |
| Posed portrait, person still | `1/125` |
| Handheld, anything | do not go below `1/60` |
| Deliberate motion blur | `1/30` or slower, camera braced |
| Panning with a runner | `1/60`, moving with them |

**The handheld rule:** below about `1/60`, your own hands shake enough to blur the shot. If you need slower,
brace the camera on something.

---

## The gym is the hard case

The gym is the single hardest place in the building to shoot, and it is where half your sports photos come
from.

The problem: you need a fast shutter for the action, but the gym is darker than it looks. Your eyes adjust.
The sensor does not.

**What to do:**

1. Set shutter to `1/500`
2. Open the aperture as wide as it goes (**lowest** f number)
3. Raise ISO until the exposure is right, `1600` or `3200` is normal indoors
4. Accept some grain. **A grainy sharp photo beats a clean blurry one.** The blurry one is unusable, the
   grainy one prints fine.

---

## Panning

Move the camera **with** the subject while the shutter is open. Done right: the subject is sharp and the
background is streaked. It looks fast in a way a frozen shot does not.

1. Set shutter to about `1/60`
2. Find the subject **before** they reach you
3. Turn your whole upper body, not just your arms
4. Keep turning **through** the shot. Do not stop when you hear the click.

**It takes a lot of tries.** Expect most of them to be unusable. The one that works is worth the roll.

## Your assignment: the Shutter Speed Pair

Same idea as the aperture pair. **One subject, two shots, one setting changed.**

| Shot | Setting | Should show |
|------|---------|-------------|
| **A: Frozen** | Fast, `1/500` or faster | Motion stopped, sharp |
| **B: Blurred** | Slow, `1/30` or slower | Visible motion blur |

**Requirements:**

- **The same subject in both.** Something actually moving: someone walking, a ball, a spinning object, hands
  working, a skateboard, a flag
- **Only the shutter speed meaningfully changed.** You may adjust ISO or aperture to keep the brightness
  right, but the subject and framing stay the same
- Note the **actual shutter speed** used for each
- **One sentence:** which one belongs in the yearbook and why

That last sentence is the real assignment. Both shots are correct. The question is which one tells the story
of this moment better, and you have to defend it.
